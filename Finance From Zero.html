            Lesson 1 · Part 1: How money works

            What is money?

            In one breath: money is anything everyone agrees to accept in exchange for things. That shared agreement is the whole trick. It lets you sell your work to one person and spend the result on something from someone completely different, without hunting for a perfect swap.

            About 25 minutesNeeds: nothingYou will learn: what a price is

              A village with no money

              Like you’re five

              You grow rice and you want a haircut. The barber does not want rice. He wants fish. So you must find a fisher who wants rice, swap your rice for fish, and then take the fish to the barber. If the fisher does not want rice, you are stuck. A swap only works when you want what I have and I want what you have. Economists call this the double coincidence of wants.

              The real idea

              Barter means swapping goods directly, with no money. It works in a tiny village where everyone knows everyone. It falls apart as soon as there are many kinds of goods and many people, because the chance that two strangers happen to want each other’s goods is small.

              What money changes

              With money, the fisher does not need to want rice. He just needs to want coins, and everyone does. You sell rice to anyone who likes rice, get coins, then buy the haircut from the barber with those coins. One extra step (coins in the middle) turns a rare double match into two easy single matches.

              The three jobs of money

              1 · Medium of exchange

              The thing in the middle of every trade. You pay with it and the seller accepts it because they can spend it too.

              2 · Unit of account

              A common measuring stick. A fish costs 10 coins, a haircut costs 50 coins. Now you can compare any two things at a glance.

              3 · Store of value

              A way to carry today’s work into the future. You earn coins in summer and still have them to spend in winter.

              The second job is easy to overlook, so here is what it saves you. Without a measuring stick, every pair of goods needs its own exchange rate: “one fish = three bowls of rice”, “one haircut = five fish”, and so on.

              Quant view

              Draw each good as a point and each exchange rate as a line between two points. A barter economy needs a line between every pair: a complete graph with n(n−1)/2 edges. Money is a hub that every good connects to: a star graph with n−1 edges. Choosing the hub as the thing you measure all prices in is called choosing a numeraire. You will meet the numeraire again when pricing options (Lesson 14), where picking a clever one makes hard problems easy.

              Trap

              Store of value is the weakest of the three jobs. A coin keeps its number forever but not its buying power, if prices rise. That is inflation, the subject of Lesson 4.

              What makes something good money?

              People have used cattle, salt, seashells, metal and paper. Some work better than others. Good money tends to be durable (does not rot), portable (you can carry it), divisible (you can pay small amounts), hard to fake, widely accepted, and stable in value. Pick a candidate and see how it scores.

              Think about it

              No candidate scores full marks on everything, which is why money keeps changing form. Notice that “widely accepted” is circular: something is accepted because it is accepted. Money is a convention, and conventions can shift.

              From shells to gold to paper to numbers

              Money has gone through a few big changes. Tap each step.

              Why does worthless-looking paper work?

              Modern money is fiat money (“fiat” is Latin for “let it be done”). It is not backed by gold. It works because of three things at once: the government declares it legal tender for debts, the government demands taxes in it so everyone needs some, and everyone else already accepts it. Its value is protected by a central bank that controls how much is created (Lesson 4).

              In the real world: the Hong Kong dollar

              HK dollar banknotes are issued by three commercial banks (HSBC, Standard Chartered and Bank of China (Hong Kong)) under the supervision of the Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA). Since 1983 the HKD has been linked to the US dollar. Under today’s Linked Exchange Rate System, the HKMA keeps the rate within a band of HK$7.75 to HK$7.85 per US$1. That is a promise about the price of one money in terms of another.

              Most money is just numbers

              Like you’re five

              When you tap your phone to pay for lunch, no coin moves anywhere. A computer subtracts a number from your balance and adds the same number to the café’s balance. Money today is mostly entries in a ledger.

              In most economies, physical cash is only a small slice of all money. Most of it is balances in bank accounts. (Lesson 2 shows where those balances come from.) Try a few payments below and watch what stays constant.

              Quant view

              Keep the balances in a vector b. A payment of x from account i to account j changes it by b ← b + x(ej − ei). The added vector has entries that sum to zero, so the total 1Tb never changes. This conservation law is why accountants write every transaction on two sides, debit and credit (double-entry bookkeeping). Every exchange, clearing system and trading platform you will study is a ledger that enforces this rule.

              Different countries, different money

              The real idea

              An exchange rate is the price of one money in another money. If US$1 costs HK$7.8, then HK$1 costs about US$0.128. Because a bank or exchange shop wants to earn a living, it buys a currency from you at a lower price than it sells it to you. The gap is the spread. It is your first look at a cost that appears in every market (Lesson 7).

              In the real world

              Rates in the demo are round numbers for illustration only, not live prices. Real quotes change every second. Airport booths often charge spreads of several percent, banks charge less, and large institutions trade at a tiny fraction of a percent.

              Quick check

              Must-know list

              • Barter needs a double coincidence of wants. Money removes it.
              • Money’s three jobs: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value.
              • With n goods, barter needs n(n−1)/2 prices; a common money needs n−1.
              • Fiat money works through legal tender, taxes and shared trust, not gold.
              • Most money today is numbers in bank ledgers. A payment moves a number and keeps the total unchanged.
              • An exchange rate is the price of one money in another; the spread is the gap between buy and sell prices.
              • HKD is linked to USD, in a band of 7.75 to 7.85.

              What banks really do

              In one breath: a bank borrows from people with spare money (depositors) and lends it to people who need money (borrowers), and keeps the gap between the interest it charges and the interest it pays. Because it lends out most of what it holds, it can never repay everyone at once. That is what makes banks useful, and also fragile.

              About 30 minutesNeeds: Lesson 1You will learn: assets, liabilities, leverage

                Why banks exist

                Like you’re five

                You keep your savings in a shoebox. It might get stolen, and it earns nothing. Your neighbour wants to open a bakery but has no money for an oven. A bank is the trusted person in the middle. It keeps your money safe, lends some of it to the baker, and pays you a little for letting it. The baker pays the bank a bit more. The bank keeps the difference.

                The real idea

                A bank is a financial intermediary. Four jobs make it worth having:

                • Matching: it connects savers with borrowers who would never find each other.
                • Size change: it pools thousands of small deposits into one big loan.
                • Time change: it takes deposits that can be withdrawn any day and lends them for years (this is called maturity transformation, and it is the root of bank fragility).
                • Checking borrowers: it studies who is likely to repay, so depositors do not have to.

                The bank’s balance sheet

                The real idea

                Every company, and every bank, can be summarised in one table called a balance sheet. The left side lists assets: what it owns or is owed (cash, loans). The right side lists liabilities: what it owes to others, plus equity: what is left for the owners. The two sides always match:

                Assets = Liabilities + Equity

                For a bank, your deposit is a liability. The bank owes you that money. A loan it made is an asset. It is owed that money.

                Run a tiny bank yourself. Press the buttons in any order and watch both sides move together.

                Trap

                Cash is an asset of the bank but a deposit is a liability. When you say “the bank has my money”, the bank would say “the bank owes someone, and has used most of it to make loans”.

                How banks make money

                A bank earns interest on its loans and pays interest on its deposits. The difference, as a percentage of what it lends, is the net interest margin. The catch is that some borrowers do not repay.

                Think about it

                Banks earn small margins on huge amounts of money. A 1% loss rate on loans can cut a 2% margin in half. That is why banks spend so much on judging who gets a loan, and why they care so much about whether the economy is healthy.

                Where does new money come from?

                Like you’re five

                You deposit HK$1,000. The bank keeps HK$100 and lends HK$900 to a baker. The baker pays a supplier, who deposits HK$900 in the same bank. Now the bank owes depositors HK$1,000 + HK$900, but there is still only HK$1,000 of original cash. Money seems to have grown from nothing, just from lending and re-depositing.

                Quant view

                Each round multiplies by (1 − r), so total deposits form a geometric series:

                Σ = D (1 + (1−r) + (1−r)2 + …) = Dr

                With r = 10% the limit is 10 times the original deposit. In practice this is a teaching toy. A modern bank does not wait for cash to arrive. When it approves a loan it simply credits the borrower’s account: a new loan (asset) and a new deposit (liability) appear together. The real limits are the bank’s capital, the demand for loans, the regulator’s rules and the central bank’s interest rate (Lesson 4).

                Bank runs

                Like you’re five

                The bank has your money in a loan to a baker, not in a drawer. If one person asks for their money, no problem. If everyone asks on the same day, the bank cannot pay, because the baker cannot hand back the oven. And if you hear everyone else is rushing to withdraw, the sensible thing for you is to rush as well, even if the bank was fine yesterday.

                Quant view

                A bank run is a coordination game with two equilibria. If you expect others to stay, staying is best. If you expect others to run, running is best. Both are self-fulfilling. This is the idea of the Diamond–Dybvig model (1983), a classic starting point for the economics of banking.

                What stops runs
                • Deposit insurance: if the government guarantees small deposits, there is no reason to run.
                • Lender of last resort: the central bank lends cash to a solvent bank that cannot get it elsewhere.
                • Rules: banks must keep minimum capital (equity) and enough liquid assets.
                In the real world

                Hong Kong’s Deposit Protection Scheme protects each depositor up to HK$800,000 per bank (check the current limit on the Deposit Protection Board’s website). In March 2023 customers of Silicon Valley Bank in the US tried to withdraw about US$42 billion in a single day, and regulators closed the bank. Most of the money was in long-dated bonds that had lost value, and news spread faster than ever on social media.

                Leverage: why small losses are big

                Like you’re five

                You buy a HK$100 toy using HK$10 of your own money and HK$90 borrowed. If the toy’s price falls to HK$90, you have lost HK$10: all your own money, from a price fall of only 10%. Using borrowed money magnifies both gains and losses.

                The real idea

                Leverage = assets ÷ equity. A bank with HK$100 of assets and HK$5 of equity has leverage of 20×. Banks are some of the most leveraged firms around, which is exactly why they are regulated.

                Quant view

                With asset return rA, borrowing cost rD and leverage L = A/E, the return on equity is

                ROE = L · rA − (L − 1) · rD

                It is linear in L: the expected return grows with leverage, but so does the standard deviation, and you are wiped out whenever rA falls below −1/L. Hedge funds, traders using margin, and banks all face this same equation. Risk management is largely the art of choosing L.

                What else banks do

                “Bank” covers several different businesses. This matters because jobs in finance sit on different sides of them.

                KindWhat it doesExamples
                Retail and commercial bankTakes deposits, makes loans, runs payments and cards.HSBC, Hang Seng Bank, Bank of China (Hong Kong)
                Investment bank / brokerHelps companies raise money by selling shares and bonds, advises on mergers, trades for clients and itself.Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, CITIC Securities
                Central bankBank for the banks. Controls interest rates and the money supply (Lesson 4).Hong Kong Monetary Authority, US Federal Reserve

                Banks that help clients trade, quote prices and sell products are called the sell side. Funds that invest money for themselves or for clients are the buy side (see the table on the home page).

                Quick check

                Must-know list

                • A bank intermediates: it connects savers and borrowers, and transforms size and time.
                • Assets = Liabilities + Equity. A deposit is a liability. A loan is an asset.
                • Profit comes from the interest margin, minus loan losses.
                • Textbook multiplier: total deposits = D / r. In reality banks create deposits when they lend, limited by capital and demand.
                • A bank run happens because deposits are short-term and loans are long-term. Insurance and central-bank lending stop it.
                • Leverage = assets / equity. A fall of 1/L in asset value wipes out the equity.

                Interest and the time value of money

                In one breath: HK$100 today is worth more than HK$100 next year, because today’s money can be put to work and earn interest. Finance is largely a machine for moving money through time, and the exchange rate between today’s money and tomorrow’s is the interest rate. Nearly every price in this course comes from this one idea.

                About 40 minutesNeeds: Lessons 1 to 2You will learn: compounding, present value, NPV

                  Why is money today worth more?

                  Like you’re five

                  A friend offers you a choice: HK$100 right now, or HK$100 in a year. You take it now. Why? You could lend it out and get HK$105 back. Or prices may rise so HK$100 buys less next year. Or your friend might forget. Interest is the rent you pay for using someone else’s money.

                  The real idea

                  An interest rate is the price of moving money across time. It covers three things: what you could earn elsewhere (opportunity), rising prices (inflation, Lesson 4) and the chance you are not repaid (risk). Everything in finance that involves the future uses it.

                  Simple and compound interest

                  Simple interest is paid only on the original amount. Compound interest is paid on the original amount plus all the interest earned so far, so interest earns interest. Drag the sliders and watch the two curves separate.

                  Vn = P(1 + r)n   (compound)    vs    V = P(1 + rn)   (simple)
                  Quant view

                  Compound growth is exponential. If money grows at rate r continuously, then dV/dt = rV, and the solution is V = V0 ert. Adding interest m times a year gives (1 + r/m)mt, which tends to ert as m → ∞. Taking logs, ln V = ln V0 + rt is a straight line, which is why finance works with log returns (Lesson 8). Continuous rates convert with rc = ln(1 + r).

                  Trap

                  A quoted rate is not always the rate you actually earn. “12% a year, paid monthly” is 1% a month, which compounds to 12.68% over a year. The rate that includes this effect is the effective annual rate. Always ask how often interest is added.

                  The rule of 72

                  The real idea

                  A handy shortcut: years to double ≈ 72 ÷ rate (in %). At 6% your money doubles in about 12 years. At 9%, about 8 years.

                  Quant view

                  Doubling time solves (1 + r)t = 2, so t = ln 2 / ln(1 + r). For small r, ln(1 + r) ≈ r − r2/2, so t ≈ 0.693 / r. The number 72 sits slightly above 69.3 to correct for the r2 term at typical rates (6% to 10%), and 72 divides neatly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12.

                  Present value: bringing the future back to today

                  Like you’re five

                  Growing money forward answers “what will my HK$100 become?”. Present value goes the other way: “what is a promise of HK$105 next year worth today?” If a bank pays 5%, HK$100 today becomes HK$105. So the promise is worth exactly HK$100 today. We say the HK$105 is discounted back at 5%.

                  PV = FV(1 + r)n = FV × d    where d = (1 + r)−n is the discount factor
                  In the real world

                  This is how a bond is priced (Lesson 5), how a share is valued (Lesson 11) and how an option is priced (Lesson 14): add up what you expect to receive, each discounted back to today. The discount factor d is just today’s price of HK$1 delivered in n years. A contract that promises exactly that is called a zero-coupon bond.

                  Many payments: NPV and IRR

                  Real decisions have cash flows in several years. Discount each one to today and add them up. That total is the net present value (NPV). If it is positive, the project creates value after paying for the time its money is tied up.

                  NPV = Σt=0T CFt(1 + r)t
                  Quant view

                  NPV is a dot product: the cash-flow vector CF times the discount-factor vector d, with dt = (1 + r)−t. Pricing anything in finance is this operation with the right cash flows and the right discount factors. The IRR is the root of NPV(r) = 0, a polynomial equation in 1/(1 + r). It can have several roots when the sign of the cash flows changes more than once (Descartes’ rule of signs), which is one reason professionals prefer NPV.

                  Trap

                  A higher IRR does not always mean a better project. A small project with an enormous IRR can add less value than a big one with a modest IRR. Compare NPVs at your true discount rate.

                  Loans: a stream of equal payments

                  A mortgage is the same idea reversed. You receive a lump sum now and promise equal payments for years. Each payment covers that month’s interest first and repays the loan with whatever is left, so at the start almost everything is interest.

                  Quant view

                  With monthly rate i and n payments, the payment M must make the present value of all payments equal the loan P. That is a geometric series:

                  P = M 1 − (1+i)−ni   ⇒   M = P i1 − (1+i)−n

                  The remaining balance after k payments is the present value of the payments still to come. This “balance = PV of the remaining flows” idea reappears for bonds.

                  In the real world

                  Many Hong Kong mortgages are priced as a margin over an interbank rate called HIBOR or over the bank’s prime rate, so the rate (and your payment) moves when the market moves. Lesson 4 explains what moves those rates.

                  Quick check

                  Must-know list

                  • Interest is the price of money over time. It covers opportunity, inflation and risk.
                  • Compound: V = P(1 + r)n. Continuous: V = Pert.
                  • Effective annual rate accounts for how often interest is added.
                  • Rule of 72: years to double ≈ 72 / rate (%).
                  • Present value: PV = FV / (1 + r)n. A higher discount rate gives a lower PV.
                  • NPV = sum of discounted cash flows. Accept projects with NPV greater than zero. IRR is the rate where NPV = 0.
                  • A level loan payment makes the PV of all payments equal the loan.

                  Inflation, central banks and the economy

                  In one breath: prices tend to drift upward (inflation), so what matters is what money can buy (real value), not the number printed on it (nominal value). Central banks steer the economy by setting a short-term interest rate, and that one rate ripples into the price of everything you will meet in this course.

                  About 35 minutesNeeds: Lessons 1 to 3You will learn: real rates, policy rates, the cycle

                    Inflation: prices drift up

                    Like you’re five

                    When your grandparents were young a bowl of noodles cost a few dollars. Now it costs much more. The noodles did not get better. Money got weaker. Inflation is the general rise in prices over time, which means each dollar buys a little less than it used to.

                    The real idea

                    To measure inflation, statisticians price a fixed shopping basket of typical goods and services and track its cost. That cost, expressed as an index, is the Consumer Price Index (CPI). Inflation is the percentage change in the CPI over a year. Typical causes: demand growing faster than supply (demand-pull), rising costs of energy or wages (cost-push), and people expecting prices to rise, which makes them ask for higher pay and set higher prices (expectations).

                    Trap

                    Inflation of 3% does not mean prices rose 3% in total. It means prices rise 3% each year, and the increases compound, exactly like interest in Lesson 3.

                    Nominal and real

                    The real idea

                    A nominal number is measured in money as it is today. A real number has been adjusted for inflation, so it measures buying power. If your savings earn 5% and prices rise 3%, you are really only about 2% better off.

                    (1 + i) = (1 + r)(1 + π)   ⇒   r = 1 + i1 + π − 1 ≈ i − π
                    Quant view

                    This is the Fisher equation. In logs it is exactly additive: ln(1 + i) = ln(1 + r) + ln(1 + π), which is why the “subtract inflation” shortcut works. The distinction that matters for pricing is between expected and realised inflation. A bond’s real return depends on inflation that has not happened yet. Inflation-linked government bonds exist precisely so the market can reveal what the real rate is.

                    Central banks and the policy rate

                    Like you’re five

                    Think of the economy as a room and the central bank as the person holding the thermostat. If the room gets too hot (prices rising too fast), they turn the heating down by making borrowing more expensive. If it gets too cold (jobs disappearing), they turn it up by making borrowing cheaper. The dial they hold is the policy rate.

                    The real idea

                    A central bank is the bank for the banks. It issues the base money that banks settle payments with and sets the policy rate, the interest rate it pays or charges on its dealings with banks. That rate feeds into every other rate:

                    policy rate → bank lending and deposit rates → bond yields → mortgage and business borrowing costs → spending and investment → inflation and jobs

                    Its other jobs: act as lender of last resort in a crisis (Lesson 2), supervise banks, and keep the currency and prices stable. For example, the US Federal Reserve aims for maximum employment and stable prices (a common inflation target is 2%), and the Hong Kong Monetary Authority’s main job is to keep the exchange-rate link steady.

                    Quant view

                    The Taylor rule (1993) is a simple feedback rule: i = r* + π + 0.5(π − π*) + 0.5y, where r* is the neutral real rate, π* the inflation target and y the output gap. It is a controller, with the policy rate as the control input and inflation and output as the state. The coefficient on π is above 1 so that real rates rise when inflation rises (the Taylor principle); otherwise the system is unstable. Central banks do not follow it mechanically, but market traders use versions of it to guess the next move.

                    In the real world

                    Hong Kong cannot set its own rates freely. With the HKD pegged to the USD and money free to move in and out, interest rates in Hong Kong mostly follow US rates. Economists call this the impossible trinity: a country cannot have a fixed exchange rate, free capital flows and an independent interest-rate policy all at once. It must give up one.

                    Risk-free rate

                    In finance you will constantly use a risk-free rate: the return on the safest asset available, such as a short-term government bill or an overnight interbank rate. It is the baseline for discounting (Lesson 3) and for judging whether a risky investment earned enough (Lessons 8 to 10). In Hong Kong the interbank benchmark is called HIBOR; in the US dollar market it is SOFR.

                    GDP and the business cycle

                    The real idea

                    Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total value of all the final goods and services an economy produces in a period. When it grows, incomes and profits tend to grow. When it falls for a couple of quarters, people call it a recession. The economy moves in waves around a long-run trend, called the business cycle. Tap each phase.

                    Think about it

                    Stock markets look forward. They tend to fall before a recession begins and start recovering before the economy does. Prices react to what is expected next, not to what is happening now. This idea returns in Lesson 15 on efficient markets.

                    Why this matters to a trader

                    In the real world

                    Markets move on surprises, not on news itself. If everyone expects the central bank to raise rates by 0.25% and it does, little happens, because the move was already in prices. If it raises by 0.50%, bond prices, stock prices and currencies can jump within seconds. Scheduled data releases (inflation, jobs, GDP) and central-bank meetings are some of the most traded events of the month. Another tool is quantitative easing (QE): when the policy rate is near zero, the central bank buys bonds with newly created reserves to push long-term rates down.

                    Quick check

                    Must-know list

                    • Inflation = general rise in prices, measured by the % change in the CPI basket.
                    • Real = adjusted for inflation. r ≈ i − π (exactly (1+i)/(1+π) − 1).
                    • The policy rate is the central bank’s main tool. Higher rate: cooler economy, lower inflation. Lower rate: the reverse.
                    • Taylor rule: i = r* + π + 0.5(π − π*) + 0.5y.
                    • The risk-free rate is the baseline return (HIBOR, SOFR, government bills).
                    • Impossible trinity: fixed exchange rate, free capital flows, independent policy: pick two. Hong Kong picks the first two.
                    • GDP and the business cycle. Markets react to surprises and look ahead.

                    Bonds: lending, prices and yields

                    In one breath: a bond is an IOU that pays you fixed amounts on fixed dates. Its price today is just the present value (Lesson 3) of those payments, discounted at whatever rate the market currently demands. So when interest rates rise, the price of existing bonds falls, and that single fact drives most of fixed-income finance.

                    About 45 minutesNeeds: Lessons 3 and 4You will learn: price, yield, duration, the yield curve

                      What is a bond?

                      Like you’re five

                      You lend your friend HK$1,000. They promise to pay you HK$50 every year as a thank-you, and to give the HK$1,000 back after five years. If your friend writes that promise on a piece of paper that anyone can buy from you, that paper is a bond. A bond is a loan that has been turned into something that can be traded.

                      The real idea

                      Governments and companies need big sums of money. Instead of borrowing from one bank, they split the loan into many small pieces and sell them to the public. Every bond is described by four numbers:

                      • Face value (also called par or principal): the amount repaid at the end, for example HK$1,000.
                      • Coupon rate: the yearly interest as a percentage of face value. A 5% coupon on HK$1,000 pays HK$50 a year. (Many bonds pay half of it every six months. We use yearly payments to keep the arithmetic clean.)
                      • Maturity: the date the loan ends and the face value is repaid.
                      • Issuer: who borrowed. A government, a bank, a company.

                      Because the payments are fixed in advance, bonds are called fixed-income securities. Try it: choose a coupon and a maturity and see the exact stream of money the bond promises.

                      In the real world

                      The biggest issuers are governments. US Treasury bonds are the world’s benchmark safe asset. In Hong Kong, the HKMA issues Exchange Fund Bills and Notes, and the Government has sold retail bonds such as the iBond and the Silver Bond to ordinary savers. Companies such as banks, airlines and property developers issue corporate bonds. The global bond market is bigger than the global stock market.

                      Why a bond’s price moves

                      Like you’re five

                      You own a bond that pays 4% a year. Tomorrow the government starts selling brand-new bonds that pay 6%. Would anyone pay full price for your 4% bond when a 6% one is on the shelf? No. To sell yours you would have to give a discount. The reverse also holds: if new bonds only pay 2%, your 4% bond becomes precious and you can sell it for more than you paid. When rates go up, old bonds go down. When rates go down, old bonds go up. Like a see-saw.

                      The real idea

                      A bond is a set of future payments, and Lesson 3 told us how to value future payments: discount each one at the going rate and add them up. The going rate for bonds of that type and maturity is the market yield y.

                      P = Σt=1n C(1 + y)t + F(1 + y)n

                      Here C is the yearly coupon, F the face value and n the number of years. Three cases, using the coupon rate as the benchmark:

                      • coupon rate = yield: price equals face value. Called trading at par.
                      • coupon rate < yield: price below face value, at a discount.
                      • coupon rate > yield: price above face value, at a premium.

                      Bond prices are quoted per 100 of face value, so a quote of 92.28 means HK$92.28 for every HK$100 of face.

                      Trap

                      “Bonds are safe” means the issuer is very likely to pay you back. It does not mean the price stays put. A 30-year bond with a 4% coupon loses about 15% of its value if the yield rises from 4% to 5%. If you must sell before maturity, you take that loss. If you hold to maturity and the issuer pays, the price wobbles do not matter, because you simply collect the promised payments.

                      Quant view

                      The price–yield relationship is decreasing and convex: P(y) is a sum of terms c(1+y)−t, each with a negative first derivative and a positive second derivative. Convexity means a fall in yield raises the price by more than the same rise in yield lowers it. That asymmetry is a free gift to the bondholder, and the market charges for it, which is part of why long bonds yield a bit less than a straight line would suggest.

                      Yield to maturity: the bond’s true rate

                      Like you’re five

                      The coupon is what the bond says it pays. But you may have bought the bond for less (or more) than its face value, so what you actually earn is different. The yield to maturity (YTM) answers: “counting everything I pay and everything I receive, what yearly rate of interest am I really getting?”

                      The real idea

                      The YTM is the single discount rate y that makes the present value of all the promised payments equal to the bond’s current price. In other words, you know the price P and solve the pricing equation for y. (It is the same as the IRR from Lesson 3, applied to the bond.) There is no neat formula. We find it by trial and error: guess a yield, compute the price, and adjust. The best way to see how is the bisection method, which you may know from programming. Press Step repeatedly.

                      Trap

                      Coupon rate and yield are different things. The coupon is fixed forever when the bond is issued. The yield changes every second with the price. A 4% coupon bond that costs 90 yields more than 4%, because you pay 90 and get back 100.

                      Quant view

                      Solving P(y) − Pmarket = 0 is one-dimensional root finding. Because P is monotonic in y, bisection always works. Newton’s method is much faster: yk+1 = yk + (P(yk) − Pmkt) / (D*P), where D* is the duration, introduced next. Trading systems run this millions of times a day. Note the convention: the quoted yield assumes you can reinvest every coupon at the same y, which is an approximation.

                      Duration: how hard does the price wobble?

                      Like you’re five

                      Imagine each payment of the bond is a weight on a long ruler, placed at the year it arrives. Heavier weights are bigger payments. Where would you put your finger to balance the ruler? That balance point is the bond’s duration. A bond that pays you back soon has its balance point near the start. A bond with a distant big payment has a balance point far to the right. And the further right, the more the bond’s value shakes when interest rates move.

                      The real idea

                      Macaulay duration is the average time you wait for your money, where each payment’s time is weighted by its present value:

                      D = Σ t · PV(CFt)P   and   D* = D1 + y   (modified duration)

                      The useful fact: D* tells you the percentage price change for a small change in yield.

                      ΔPP ≈ −D* · Δy   +   ½ C (Δy)2

                      So a bond with D* = 8 loses about 8% when yields rise by 1 percentage point. The second term is convexity (C), a correction for the curve bending. Two rules of thumb: a longer maturity and a lower coupon both mean a larger duration. A zero-coupon bond’s duration is exactly its maturity.

                      Quant view

                      Duration is just a derivative. If P(y) is the price, then D* = −P′(y)/P and convexity is P″(y)/P. The first-order estimate is the tangent line, and adding the second-order term gives the parabola that hugs the curve. This is a Taylor expansion, exactly what you met in calculus. Traders quote risk in DV01: the money you lose when yields rise by one basis point (1 bp = 0.01%). For a position of HK$10 million with D* = 8, DV01 ≈ 10,000,000 × 8 × 0.0001 = HK$8,000. When we reach options in Lesson 14, the “Greeks” (delta, gamma) will be the same idea with different names.

                      The yield curve

                      Like you’re five

                      If you lend money for a week, a month or thirty years, would you ask for the same interest? Probably not. Lending for longer is riskier and ties up your money. So the market offers a menu of rates, one for each length of loan. Draw that menu as a line and you get the yield curve.

                      The real idea

                      The cleanest bond to study is a zero-coupon bond: no coupons, just one payment of face value at maturity. Its price is just F × d, where d = 1/(1+s)T is the discount factor from Lesson 3 and s is the spot rate for maturity T. The yield curve is the plot of spot rates against maturity.

                      The key insight: every coupon bond is a bundle of zero-coupon bonds, one for each payment date. So its price is the sum of each payment times the discount factor of its own date:

                      P = Σ CFt · dt

                      Four shapes you will hear about: normal (rising: long-term lending pays more), steep (rising sharply, often early in a recovery), flat, and inverted (short-term rates above long-term, a warning sign).

                      In the real world

                      The US Treasury curve has inverted before most US recessions since the 1960s, so analysts watch the gap between the 10-year and 2-year yields closely. It is a clue, not a law: the warning can come early, and there have been false alarms. Central banks (Lesson 4) control the short end through the policy rate. Expectations and risk shape the long end.

                      Quant view

                      Given prices of liquid bonds you can solve backwards for the discount factors. This is bootstrapping. Once you have the curve, the market also implies forward rates: the rate for borrowing between two future dates. No-arbitrage ties them together: (1 + s2)2 = (1 + s1)(1 + f1,2). If a bond’s market price differed from Σ CF·d, you could buy the cheap one, sell the replicating bundle and pocket the difference. That idea (arbitrage-free pricing) is the foundation of derivatives pricing, so keep it in mind.

                      Credit risk and the spread

                      Like you’re five

                      You would happily lend to your mum without worrying. You would ask a bit more interest from a stranger who has missed payments before. The extra interest is the price of the chance that they will not pay you back.

                      The real idea

                      A government that borrows in its own currency can always print more, so its risk is low. Companies can fail, which is called default. Investors demand a credit spread: extra yield above the risk-free rate. Agencies (S&P, Moody’s, Fitch) give ratings. From safest to riskiest: AAA, AA, A, BBB (these are investment grade), then BB, B, CCC and below (high yield, or “junk”). If a bond does default, the holder usually recovers part of the money, the recovery rate. A fair spread roughly covers the expected yearly loss:

                      spread ≈ p × (1 − R)   (p = default probability per year, R = recovery rate)
                      In the real world

                      Hong Kong’s market has seen credit risk up close. Several large China property developers that had sold dollar bonds to international investors, Evergrande among them, stopped paying in 2021, and bondholders recovered only a fraction. When a bond’s default looks likely, its price collapses even before the missed payment, so “high yield” is high for a reason.

                      Quant view

                      Real spreads are usually larger than p(1 − R) implied by actual default history. The extra part pays for bearing risk (defaults cluster in bad times, when losses hurt most) and for illiquidity. Credit quants therefore distinguish real-world default probabilities from the risk-neutral ones implied by prices. We will meet the risk-neutral idea properly in Lesson 14.

                      Why this matters to a trader

                      In the real world

                      Three reasons you cannot skip bonds. First, the risk-free yield curve is the discount rate used to value nearly everything else: stocks (Lesson 11), futures (Lesson 12) and options (Lesson 14). Second, bond yields are a market-implied forecast of interest rates, which feeds currency, stock and credit trading. Third, fixed income is a huge field for quants: curve modelling, rates derivatives, relative-value trades between bonds that should be priced consistently.

                      Quick check

                      Must-know list

                      • A bond = fixed payments: coupons each year plus face value at maturity. Issued by governments and companies.
                      • Price = present value of the payments at the market yield: P = Σ C/(1+y)t + F/(1+y)n.
                      • Yields up, prices down. Coupon < yield: discount. Coupon > yield: premium. Equal: par.
                      • YTM = the discount rate that makes PV equal the price. Found numerically (bisection, Newton).
                      • Duration = PV-weighted average time to payment. ΔP/P ≈ −D*Δy + ½C(Δy)². Longer maturity and lower coupon mean larger duration.
                      • Yield curve = spot rates by maturity. Coupon bond price = Σ CF × discount factor. Inversion is a recession signal.
                      • Credit spread ≈ p(1 − R) plus a risk premium. Bonds are safe about repayment, not about price.
                      • 1 basis point = 0.01%. DV01 = money lost per +1 bp.

                      Stocks, indices and ETFs

                      In one breath: a stock is a tiny slice of ownership in a company. You share in its profits (dividends) and in its price changes. Its price is the market’s best guess of all future profits, discounted to today (Lesson 3 again). An index bundles many stocks into one number, and an ETF lets you buy that bundle in one click.

                      About 45 minutesNeeds: Lessons 3 and 5You will learn: market cap, EPS, P/E, index weighting, ETFs

                        What is a stock?

                        Like you’re five

                        Imagine your uncle owns a pizza shop and cuts its ownership into 1,000,000 equal slices. If you buy 10,000 slices, you own 1% of the shop. When the shop makes a profit, 1% of that profit is “yours”: sometimes the shop hands it out as cash, sometimes it keeps it to open a new shop. If the shop becomes famous, everyone wants slices, so slices cost more. If it burns down, slices are worth almost nothing. Those slices are shares, or stocks.

                        The real idea

                        A share is a unit of ownership (equity) in a company. Shareholders are the owners, but they come last in the queue when the company is wound up: staff, suppliers and bondholders (Lesson 5) are paid first, and the shareholders get whatever is left (the residual claim). In exchange, shareholders keep all the upside. Three more things to know:

                        • Limited liability. The most you can lose is the money you paid for the shares. You never owe more.
                        • Voting. Ordinary shares usually carry votes on big decisions, such as electing the board.
                        • Primary and secondary markets. A company raises money when it first sells shares to the public in an IPO (initial public offering). After that, shares trade between investors on an exchange, and the company receives nothing from those trades.

                        Four numbers every investor uses:

                        Market cap = price × shares outstanding   |   EPS = profitshares   |   P/E = priceEPS   |   Dividend yield = dividend per shareprice

                        Market capitalisation is what the whole company is worth at the current price. EPS is earnings (profit) per share. P/E says how many dollars investors pay for each dollar of yearly profit. A dividend is the part of profit paid out to shareholders as cash. Play with a fictional company that has 10 million shares.

                        Trap

                        A low share price does not mean a stock is cheap, and a high one does not mean it is expensive. A HK$5 share of a huge company can be pricier than a HK$500 share of a small one. Size is the market cap. Cheapness is measured by ratios like P/E, not by the price tag.

                        In the real world

                        Hong Kong’s market is run by HKEX (Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing). Shares trade in fixed board lots, for example 100 or 500 shares at a time, which differs by company. Trading happens in a morning and an afternoon session. Hong Kong also lists many mainland Chinese companies, and some are listed in both Hong Kong and Shanghai or Shenzhen. In mainland China and Hong Kong charts, red means price up and green means down, the reverse of the US convention. This app uses green for gains, so check the colours whenever you read a chart.

                        Why do stock prices move?

                        Like you’re five

                        A price is just what the last buyer and seller agreed on. If more people want to buy than sell, the price climbs until enough sellers appear. People want to buy when they think the company will earn more money in the future than others think. So a stock’s price is a vote on the future, which is why it jumps on news, even when nothing has changed in the shop yet.

                        The real idea

                        A stock is a claim on future dividends. So, exactly as for a bond, its fair price is the present value of those payments. If the next dividend is D1, it grows at g per year forever, and investors demand a return r, then the geometric series from Lesson 3 gives:

                        P = D1r − g   (needs r > g)

                        This is the Gordon growth model. It is crude, but it teaches three lessons: higher expected growth g raises the price, a higher required return r lowers it, and when r and g are close together the price becomes extremely sensitive. Slide the numbers.

                        Quant view

                        In general, P = E[Σt Dt / (1+r)t], an expectation over uncertain future dividends, with r = risk-free rate + a premium for risk (Lessons 8 to 10). Differentiating the Gordon formula, a stock behaves like a bond with a very long duration, roughly 1/(r − g): with r − g = 5%, about 20 years. That is why stocks, especially fast-growing ones, tend to fall when interest rates rise (Lesson 4): r goes up and the denominator shrinks the value.

                        Stock indices: one number for the whole market

                        Like you’re five

                        If you want to know how a class is doing in maths, you do not read every pupil’s test. You look at the class average. A stock index is the class average for a group of companies: one number that rises when the group does well and falls when it does badly.

                        The real idea

                        An index is a rule for combining many stock prices into one level, starting from a base value such as 100. The crucial design choice is the weighting:

                        • Market-cap weighted. Each company counts in proportion to its market cap. Bigger companies move the index more. This is the standard: the S&P 500 (500 large US companies) and the Hang Seng Index (the leading Hong Kong benchmark, with a base of 100 set in July 1964) work this way, with adjustments for shares actually available to trade and caps on the very largest weights.
                        • Price weighted. Each company counts in proportion to its share price. The Dow Jones Industrial Average (30 US companies) and Japan’s Nikkei 225 do this.
                        • Equal weighted. Each company counts the same.

                        The same five companies give three different indices. Pick a company, shock its price, and watch how differently each design reacts.

                        Trap

                        In a price-weighted index, the number of shares and the size of the company are ignored. If a company splits its shares 2-for-1, the price halves although nothing real changed, and its influence on a price-weighted index halves too (the index has to be adjusted with a “divisor” to avoid a jump). This is one reason market-cap weighting is preferred.

                        Quant view

                        For weights wi that sum to 1, the index return is the weighted average of the stock returns: Rindex = Σ wiRi. Cap weights are special: they are what the average investor in aggregate holds, and they drift automatically as prices move, so a cap-weighted portfolio needs almost no trading. This is the “market portfolio” that CAPM (Lesson 10) is built on. One more warning, for when you test strategies later: an index is regularly reshuffled, with winners added and losers dropped. If you test a strategy using today’s members applied to the past, you accidentally pick up survivors, which is survivorship bias (Lesson 16).

                        Index funds and ETFs

                        Like you’re five

                        Picking the best of 80 companies is hard. A fund is a big basket: you and many others put money in, and a manager buys lots of stocks with it. An index fund simply copies an index, no cleverness needed. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is an index fund wrapped so it trades like a single stock, all day, at the market price.

                        The real idea

                        Funds fall into two camps. Active funds try to beat the market by picking stocks, and charge high fees for that. Passive funds just track an index at very low cost. Fees are quoted as a yearly percentage, the expense ratio. A small-looking fee is deceptive because it is taken every year from the whole pot, so it compounds against you, exactly like interest in Lesson 3 but with a minus sign.

                        In the real world

                        Hong Kong’s best-known ETF is the Tracker Fund of Hong Kong, which follows the Hang Seng Index. It was created in 1999 so that the government could sell the shares it had bought in 1998 while defending the market. ETFs stay close to the value of the stocks inside them because large institutions, called authorised participants, can swap baskets of stocks for ETF units and back. If the ETF price drifts away from the basket value, they trade away the gap for a profit.

                        Think about it

                        If most professional managers charge high fees and still fail to beat a cheap index over long periods, what does that suggest about how hard it is to find an edge? Hold this thought for Lesson 15 on efficient markets.

                        Why this matters to a trader

                        In the real world

                        Indices are the benchmarks: whether a strategy is good depends on whether it beat its index (Lesson 10). ETFs are the easiest instruments to trade for a quant, since they give diversified exposure in one line of code and are very liquid. And a huge amount of algorithmic work happens in the cross-section of stocks: ranking hundreds of them on signals such as value (low P/E) or momentum, then buying the top and selling the bottom. You now know what a stock, its price drivers and its benchmark are.

                        Quick check

                        Must-know list

                        • A share = a slice of ownership. Shareholders are paid last (residual claim) and can lose at most what they paid (limited liability).
                        • Market cap = price × shares outstanding. EPS = profit ÷ shares. P/E = price ÷ EPS. Dividend yield = dividend per share ÷ price.
                        • Price = present value of expected future dividends. Gordon: P = D1/(r − g).
                        • Higher required return, lower price. That is how interest rates hit stocks.
                        • Index = one number summarising many stocks. Weighting choices: market-cap (S&P 500, Hang Seng), price (Dow, Nikkei), equal.
                        • ETF / index fund = a basket you can buy in one trade. Expense ratios compound against you.
                        • Colour convention: HK and mainland China show red = up.

                        How a market actually works

                        In one breath: an exchange is a matching machine. Buyers post the highest price they will pay (bids), sellers post the lowest they will accept (asks), and whenever the two meet, a trade happens. The gap between them is the spread, market makers earn it for standing ready to trade, and every real strategy you will ever build pays it. Understanding this machine is what separates a backtest from a trading system.

                        About 50 minutesNeeds: Lesson 6You will learn: order book, spread, slippage, market making, latency

                          The order book

                          Like you’re five

                          Picture a notice-board at a market. On the left, sticky notes say “I will BUY this for 49.95”. On the right, notes say “I will SELL this for 50.00”. A buyer and a seller only trade when a buy note and a sell note agree on a price. Nobody is forced to trade, so there is always a little gap between the best buy note and the best sell note. That board, kept in order, is the order book.

                          The real idea

                          Every exchange keeps, for each stock, two lists of waiting limit orders:

                          • Bids: buy orders, sorted from the highest price down. The top one is the best bid.
                          • Asks (or offers): sell orders, sorted from the lowest price up. The top one is the best ask.

                          The difference best ask − best bid is the spread. The average of the two is the mid-price. Prices move in fixed steps called ticks; for example, a Hong Kong stock priced between HK$20 and HK$100 moves in steps of HK$0.05. The amount waiting at each price is the depth, and the book as a whole is liquidity on display.

                          When a new order arrives, a matching engine decides what trades. The rule is price-time priority: better prices first, and among equal prices, whoever arrived first. A trade always happens at the price of the resting order, the one that was waiting. Below is a working toy exchange. Prices are in HK$, quantities are in lots (1 lot = 100 shares). Send some orders and watch the book change.

                          Quant view

                          You can write this in an afternoon, and it is a data-structures exercise. Each side is a priority queue keyed by price, and each price level holds a FIFO queue of orders:

                          def match(order):
    opp = asks if order.side == BUY else bids
    while order.qty > 0 and opp and crosses(order, opp.best_price):
        resting = opp.best_level.queue[0]          # earliest at the best price
        q = min(order.qty, resting.qty)
        trade(price=resting.price, qty=q)          # trades at the RESTING price
        order.qty -= q; resting.qty -= q
        if resting.qty == 0: opp.pop_front()
    if order.qty > 0 and order.type == LIMIT:
        same_side.add(order)                       # joins the back of its queue

                          Real engines keep prices in a sorted structure (a heap or balanced tree, or an array indexed by tick) so that finding the best price is O(1) and inserting is O(log n) at worst, and they process millions of messages per second. One useful feature for machine learning: order-book imbalance, (bid depth − ask depth) / (bid depth + ask depth), measured over the top few levels. When it is strongly positive, buyers are queued up heavily and the next price move tends, on average, to be up. The toy exchange shows it live.

                          In the real world

                          A trading day on HKEX is not a single continuous session. It opens with an auction, trades continuously through a morning and an afternoon session, and ends with a closing auction that sets the official closing price. In an auction, orders are collected and then all matched at one price, chosen to make the traded volume as large as possible. Index funds care a lot about that closing price.

                          Market orders, limit orders and price impact

                          Like you’re five

                          Two ways to buy an ice-cream. You can say “I will take one right now, whatever it costs”, which is a market order. Or you can say “I will only pay HK$10, and I will wait”, which is a limit order. The first guarantees you get it but not the price. The second guarantees the price but not that you get it.

                          The real idea

                          A market order trades immediately against whatever is on the other side of the book. A small one just crosses the spread: you buy at the best ask and a seller buys at the best bid, so your “cost of immediacy” is about half the spread. A big order eats several price levels, and each level is a worse price than the last. That is slippage, or price impact. A limit order rests in the book and earns the better price if it is hit, but it may never fill, and if the market runs away you miss the move completely.

                          The thinner the book, the worse the slippage. The chart shows total waiting volume (cumulative depth) at each price; a market buy walks to the right along the orange staircase.

                          Trap

                          Backtests usually assume you trade at the last price you saw. In a real market you buy at the ask and sell at the bid, and a larger order pays more. A strategy that makes 0.03% per trade before costs can easily lose money once the spread and slippage are included. The spread is a tax on every round trip, so a high-turnover strategy needs a very strong signal.

                          Quant view

                          Empirically, the price impact of a metaorder of size Q grows roughly like the square root: impact ≈ c·σ·√(Q/V), where σ is daily volatility and V daily volume. Doubling your size does not double the impact, but it still costs real money. Execution algorithms (TWAP, VWAP) slice a big order into many small ones over time to reduce it. We return to costs in Lesson 16.

                          Spreads and market makers

                          Like you’re five

                          At the airport there is a currency booth with a sign: “We buy dollars at 7.78. We sell dollars at 7.82.” They always have both a buying price and a selling price, so anybody can trade at any time. The 0.04 gap is their pay, and also their risk: if the dollar suddenly drops while they are holding a pile, they lose. That booth is a market maker.

                          The real idea

                          A market maker continuously quotes both a bid and an ask. Whoever wants to trade right now does so against them, and the maker earns about half the spread on each trade. That service is called providing liquidity. Three forces determine how wide the spread must be:

                          • Operating costs of staying in the market.
                          • Inventory risk: after trading, the maker holds stock whose price may move against them.
                          • Adverse selection: some customers know something the maker does not. If an informed trader buys from you right before the price jumps, you sold too cheap. The spread has to earn back those losses.

                          Run a trading day for a market maker who always quotes around the true price. Each customer who arrives trades one lot. Increase the share of informed traders and see how it breaks the business. Use “New random day” to try other luck.

                          Quant view

                          Per trade, the expected profit in this toy is ≈ s/2 − qσ, where s is the spread, q the share of informed traders and σ the size of the price move they know about. This is the core of the Glosten–Milgrom model (1985): the equilibrium spread is positive even with competitive makers because it compensates for trading against better-informed people. It is also why a wide spread signals that a stock’s price is uncertain or its information is concentrated, and why spreads blow out in a panic.

                          Speed: high-frequency trading

                          Like you’re five

                          Two kids race to grab the last cookie on a table. The kid sitting next to the plate wins every time, even if the other kid is smarter. When a price on the board is out of date for a split second, whoever reaches it first can take it. Some trading firms pay a fortune to sit next to the plate.

                          The real idea

                          High-frequency trading (HFT) means automated strategies that hold positions for milliseconds to seconds and make tiny profits on enormous volume. Typical jobs: market making, latency arbitrage (hitting stale quotes), and keeping related prices consistent, for instance an ETF and the stocks inside it. Speed matters so much that firms pay to place their servers in the exchange’s own data centre (co-location). Distance matters because information cannot travel faster than light, and in optical fibre light covers about 200 km in a millisecond, or 5 microseconds (µs) per km one way. Try it.

                          In the real world

                          The race has gone so far that firms build microwave and laser links between data centres because signals travel faster through air than through glass. The practical lesson for you is not to compete on speed. A person with a laptop, or a researcher with a cloud server, cannot out-run an HFT firm. A strategy that holds positions for minutes, days or weeks competes on information and modelling, not on microseconds.

                          Why this matters to a trader

                          In the real world

                          Three things carry forward. Costs: every trade pays roughly half the spread plus slippage plus fees, so you must always compare a signal’s expected profit with its trading cost. Data: Level 1 data is just the best bid and ask and the last trade; Level 2 shows depth; tick data records every order and trade and is enormous. Many ML features come from here. Realism: when you backtest later, you will have to decide at what price a trade would really have happened, and an honest answer includes the spread.

                          Quick check

                          Must-know list

                          • Order book: bids (buy limits, best = highest) and asks (sell limits, best = lowest). Spread = best ask − best bid. Mid = their average.
                          • Price-time priority: best price first, then earliest. Trades happen at the resting order’s price.
                          • Market order: immediate, price not guaranteed. Limit order: price guaranteed, fill not guaranteed.
                          • Slippage grows with order size and shrinks with depth. Every round trip pays about the full spread.
                          • Market makers earn the spread, and bear inventory risk and adverse selection (informed traders).
                          • Order-book imbalance = (bid depth − ask depth) / (total) is a standard short-term signal.
                          • HFT competes on microseconds. Light in fibre ≈ 5 µs per km. Do not compete on speed; compete on information.

                          Lesson 8 · Part 3: The maths of risk and return

                          Returns, risk and randomness

                          In one breath: prices are not what we model, returns are: the percentage change per period. Returns multiply over time, so quants use logarithms to make them add. The typical size of a return’s wiggle is the volatility, and the simplest model of prices says they take a random walk whose spread grows like the square root of time. Real markets are rougher than that, with fat tails and crashes.

                          About 55 minutesNeeds: Lessons 3 and 6, basic probabilityYou will learn: log returns, volatility, random walk, fat tails, drawdown, VaR

                            Returns, not prices

                            Like you’re five

                            A bubble tea goes from HK$20 to HK$22. A car goes from HK$200,000 to HK$202,000. The tea rose by HK$2 and the car by HK$2,000, so the car “won”, but the tea got 10% more expensive and the car only 1%. To compare things of different sizes, we do not ask “how many dollars?” but “what fraction?”. That fraction is the return.

                            The real idea

                            If you buy at price P0, sell at P1 and collect a dividend D in between, the simple return (or holding-period return) is

                            R = P1 + D − P0P0 = P1 + DP0 − 1

                            Why do quants work with returns and not prices? Returns are scale-free (a HK$5 stock and a HK$500 stock become comparable), and they hover around a roughly stable level, while prices wander off. Statistics and machine learning need that stability. A rule that works on “returns of about ±2%” can be reused on any stock. A rule about “prices near 300” is meaningless on another stock.

                            Trap

                            Gains and losses are not symmetric. If you lose 50%, you need to gain 100% to get back to where you started. The bigger the loss, the more savage the climb. Move the slider.

                            Compounding: why we use log returns

                            Like you’re five

                            You earn 10% in week one. In week two you earn another 10%, but on the bigger amount, so after two weeks you are 21% up, not 20%. Returns multiply. Multiplication is awkward, so we use a trick that turns multiplication into addition: the logarithm.

                            The real idea

                            Over several periods, growth factors multiply: 1 + Rtotal = (1 + R1)(1 + R2)⋯(1 + Rn). Take the natural log and the product becomes a sum. The log return of one period is r = ln(P1/P0) = ln(1 + R), and

                            rtotal = r1 + r2 + ⋯ + rn   (log returns add over time)

                            For small moves, r ≈ R (for R = 1%, ln 1.01 = 0.995%). The gap matters for big moves: +100% is a log return of 69.3%, and −50% is −69.3%, so doubling and halving cancel exactly in log space, as they should.

                            The deeper consequence is that the average return misleads. Suppose a stock goes up 40% one year and down 20% the next. The average is +10%, but 100 → 140 → 112, which is only +5.8% a year. This gap is called volatility drag. In the demo, a stock alternates good and bad years with the same average return. See how the real growth falls as the swings increase.

                            Quant view

                            With R having mean μ and variance σ², the typical growth rate of wealth is the geometric mean, approximately μ − σ²/2. (Derive it: ln(1+R) ≈ R − R²/2 and take expectations.) So an investor who cares about long-run wealth wants high return and low variance, and cannot ignore risk. This is the seed of the Kelly criterion for position sizing and of mean–variance thinking in Lesson 9.

                            Volatility and the random walk

                            Like you’re five

                            A drunk person walks along a street. Each step, they go a little forward or a little backward at random, with no memory of the last step. After 100 steps they are not 100 steps away, only about 10 on average: the spread grows with the square root of the number of steps. A stock price is a little like that walker, with a gentle push in one direction (the expected return).

                            The real idea

                            Volatility σ is the standard deviation of returns, the typical size of the wiggle, and the standard measure of risk. It is almost always quoted per year. If daily returns are roughly independent, variances add, so

                            σyear = σday × √252     and in general     σT = σ × √T

                            (There are about 252 trading days in a year.) Typical annual volatilities: broad stock indices roughly 15 to 20%, single stocks 25 to 50%, very speculative assets far more. The simplest model of a price is then the random walk with drift: each period the log price changes by a constant expected amount plus a random shock. Watch many possible futures of one stock.

                            Quant view

                            In continuous time this model is geometric Brownian motion (GBM): ST = S0 exp((μ − σ²/2)T + σ√T Z), with Z ~ N(0, 1). The log price is normal, so the price itself is lognormal: it can never go below zero, and the central “median” path grows at μ − σ²/2, not μ (volatility drag again). GBM is the engine inside the Black–Scholes option formula (Lesson 14). Its code is two lines: S *= exp((mu - 0.5*s**2)*dt + s*sqrt(dt)*randn()).

                            Real returns have fat tails

                            Like you’re five

                            If you measured the height of a thousand adults, almost no one would be twice the average height. Heights are well-behaved. Stock returns are not. Most days are calm, but now and then a day is far wilder than anything the bell curve would allow. Those rare shocks are called fat tails.

                            The real idea

                            The bell curve (normal distribution) says a daily move of more than 4 standard deviations should happen about once in 60 years. In real stock markets such days arrive every few years. Real returns have fat tails (high kurtosis): extreme moves are much more common than the normal model predicts, and big moves tend to cluster together (calm periods and turbulent periods). The demo draws returns from a Student-t distribution, a standard fat-tailed model, and compares them with the bell curve. A low “degrees of freedom” means fat tails; a high one approaches the normal.

                            In the real world

                            On 19 October 1987 the US S&P 500 fell about 20% in a single day. On 26 October 1987, when it reopened after a four-day closure, the Hang Seng Index fell by around a third. Under a bell curve with normal daily volatility these events would essentially never happen. Models that assume normal returns systematically underestimate crash risk, a lesson many funds learned the hard way in 2008.

                            Quant view

                            Two stylised facts every quant memorises: (1) returns are fat-tailed, and (2) volatility clusters: large moves tend to follow large moves, whereas the direction of returns is almost unpredictable. Models like GARCH capture the second fact. This is also why risk is estimated with robust, forward-looking methods rather than a single historical standard deviation.

                            Measuring risk: drawdown and VaR

                            Like you’re five

                            Volatility tells you how bumpy the road is. But what you really feel is “how far did I fall from my best point?” That is the drawdown. And a risk manager asks “on a really bad day, how much might I lose?”. That is Value at Risk.

                            The real idea

                            Drawdown at time t is the percentage fall from the highest value so far. The maximum drawdown is the worst such fall, the number that tells you how much pain a strategy inflicted. Value at Risk (VaR) at 95% over one day is the loss that is exceeded only on the worst 5% of days, the 5th percentile of the return distribution (sign flipped). Under a normal model, VaR95 ≈ 1.645σday − μday. Simulate one price path and read off its risk numbers.

                            Trap

                            VaR says nothing about how bad the days beyond the cut-off are. A 95% VaR of 2% means 1 day in 20 is worse than −2%, but it could be −2.1% or −20%. That is why risk managers also study expected shortfall, the average loss on those worst days.

                            Think about it

                            If prices really were a pure random walk, nobody could predict the next move, and every strategy would just be noise. Yet some funds make money for decades. Either they were lucky, they carry hidden risk, or the walk is not quite random. We take this question up in Lessons 15 and 16.

                            Quick check

                            Must-know list

                            • Simple return R = (P1 + D)/P0 − 1. Log return r = ln(P1/P0). Log returns add across time; simple returns multiply.
                            • To recover from a loss of L, you need a gain of L/(1 − L). A 50% loss needs a 100% gain.
                            • Volatility = standard deviation of returns. Annualise: σyear = σday√252. Spread scales as √T.
                            • Volatility drag: growth ≈ μ − σ²/2. The average return overstates what you get.
                            • Random walk / GBM: log price moves by drift plus noise. Price is lognormal and cannot go below zero.
                            • Fat tails and volatility clustering: real returns are not normal.
                            • Max drawdown = worst peak-to-trough fall. VaR = a quantile of losses. It ignores the size of the tail beyond it.

                            Portfolios and diversification

                            In one breath: a portfolio’s return is just the weighted average of its assets’ returns, but its risk is not the weighted average of their risks. Assets that do not move together cancel each other’s wiggles, so mixing them lowers risk for free. The whole story fits in one line of linear algebra, wᵀΣw, and it leads to the efficient frontier, the starting point of modern portfolio theory.

                            About 60 minutesNeeds: Lesson 8, linear algebraYou will learn: correlation, covariance matrix, diversification, efficient frontier, Sharpe ratio

                              A portfolio is a weighted average

                              Like you’re five

                              You have a basket of fruit. Some is apples, some is oranges. A portfolio is a basket of investments, and the weights say what fraction of your money sits in each. If 60% is in A and 40% is in B, the weights are 0.6 and 0.4. They must add up to 1 (100%).

                              The real idea

                              For weights w1, …, wn that sum to 1, the portfolio’s expected return is the weighted average of the assets’ expected returns:

                              μp = Σ wi μi = wTμ

                              Example: 60% in an asset expecting 10% and 40% in one expecting 5% gives 0.6 × 10 + 0.4 × 5 = 8%. There is nothing surprising here. The surprise is in the risk.

                              Correlation: do two assets move together?

                              Like you’re five

                              Imagine two shops on the beach: one sells ice cream, one sells umbrellas. On sunny days the ice-cream shop does well and the umbrella shop does badly. On rainy days it is the reverse. If you own a share of both, you are almost always fine. The two shops move in opposite directions, so their ups and downs cancel.

                              The real idea

                              The correlation ρ between two assets is a number between −1 and +1:

                              • ρ = +1: they move perfectly together. No cancellation.
                              • ρ = 0: unrelated.
                              • ρ = −1: perfect opposites. The wiggles can cancel completely.

                              The covariance is the same information in raw units: Cov(A, B) = ρ σA σB. Drag the slider to see what each value of ρ looks like for 200 pairs of daily returns.

                              In the real world

                              Two banks in the same market often have correlation of 0.7 or higher. Stocks and government bonds often sit near zero or slightly negative, but that relationship changes over time, and in some years both fell together. Correlations are not constants; they tend to jump towards +1 in crashes, exactly when you need diversification most.

                              Diversification with two assets

                              The real idea

                              For two assets with weights w and 1 − w, the portfolio variance is

                              σp² = w²σA² + (1−w)²σB² + 2w(1−w) ρ σAσB

                              The last term is the whole story. When ρ = 1 it makes σp = wσA + (1−w)σB, the plain weighted average of risks. Any ρ below 1 makes the portfolio less risky than the average of its parts. Here we mix a stock fund (9% expected return, 20% volatility) with a bond fund (4%, 6%). The curve shows every possible mix from all-bonds to all-stocks.

                              Quant view

                              Setting the derivative of σp² to zero gives the minimum-variance weight w* = (σB² − ρσAσB) / (σA² + σB² − 2ρσAσB). With ρ = −1 you can build a portfolio with exactly zero risk, and that is the intuition behind hedging. Notice that a small amount of the riskier asset can lower the risk of an all-bond portfolio: the curve bends back before it rises.

                              Many assets: what diversification cannot remove

                              Like you’re five

                              If you own only one ice-cream shop, one bad day ruins you. If you own twenty shops in twenty towns, one bad day in one town barely matters. But if the whole country has a storm, all twenty shops suffer together. You can diversify away the bad luck of single shops, but not the weather that hits everyone.

                              The real idea

                              Split risk into two parts. Idiosyncratic (or specific) risk belongs to one company: a lawsuit, a failed product, a fraud. It averages out as you add stocks. Systematic (or market) risk affects everything: recessions, interest-rate shocks, wars. It stays. For n equal-weighted stocks, each with volatility σ and average pairwise correlation ρ:

                              σp² = σ² [ 1n + (1 − 1n) ρ ]

                              As n → ∞ this tends to ρσ². The 1/n term (idiosyncratic) vanishes; the ρσ² term (systematic) is a floor. Adding more stocks helps quickly at first and then barely at all.

                              Think about it

                              Since the market-wide part of risk cannot be diversified away, would investors get paid extra for holding it? And would they get paid for taking idiosyncratic risk that they could have diversified for free? The answers (yes, and no) are the heart of CAPM in the next lesson.

                              The efficient frontier

                              Like you’re five

                              Imagine plotting every possible mix of your fruit basket on a map, with “how bumpy” sideways and “how much reward” upwards. You always prefer to be higher and further left. Most mixes are strictly worse than some other mix. The best ones form an upper-left edge, the efficient frontier. Anything below it can be improved for free.

                              The real idea

                              With n assets, a weight vector w gives

                              μp = wTμ     σp² = wT Σ w

                              where Σ is the covariance matrix: its diagonal holds the variances, its off-diagonal entries the covariances. The efficient frontier is the set of weight vectors that minimise risk for each target return (Harry Markowitz, 1952, Nobel Prize 1990). If you can also lend or borrow at a risk-free rate rf, the best risky mix is the one with the highest Sharpe ratio, the reward per unit of risk:

                              Sharpe = μp − rfσp

                              Everyone who accepts this logic holds the same risky mix and then dials the overall risk up or down by mixing it with cash (the dashed line, called the capital allocation line). The demo has four illustrative assets (the numbers are made up for teaching, not forecasts). Set your own weights, or jump to the frontier’s special points.

                              Trap

                              Optimisers are error maximisers. The inputs, especially expected returns, are only estimates, and the optimiser loves any asset whose estimate happens to be too high. Switch the demo to “nudged by ±1%”: a one-point change in expected returns, far below what anyone can estimate, moves the best mix a lot. That is why professionals shrink estimates, cap positions, or avoid expected returns entirely (minimum-variance and risk-parity portfolios use only Σ).

                              Quant view

                              Without a ban on short selling and with a risk-free asset, the optimum has a closed form: w* ∝ Σ−1(μ − rf1). Two linear-algebra points. First, Σ is symmetric and positive semi-definite, so it has an eigen-decomposition, Σ = VΛVT. The eigenvectors are uncorrelated “principal portfolios”, and for stocks the first one is roughly “the market”, carrying most of the variance. This is PCA, and it is the foundation of factor models (Lesson 10). Second, with n = 500 stocks you must estimate n(n+1)/2 = 125,250 variances and covariances from a few hundred days of data, so the estimated Σ is noisy and often nearly singular. Standard remedies: shrinkage (Ledoit–Wolf), factor models, and regularised or hierarchical methods.

                              Why this matters to a trader

                              In the real world

                              Every fund you will ever build is a portfolio, so wᵀΣw is everywhere: risk budgeting (how much risk each position adds), hedging (choosing a position whose correlation with your book is negative), pairs trading (go long one stock and short a highly correlated one, so the common market part cancels), and position sizing (small, uncorrelated bets add up to a smoother result). A strategy’s true risk comes from correlations between its bets, not from the number of bets.

                              Quick check

                              Must-know list

                              • Portfolio return = weighted average: μp = wTμ. Weights sum to 1.
                              • Portfolio variance = wTΣw. Two assets: σp² = w²σA² + (1−w)²σB² + 2w(1−w)ρσAσB.
                              • Correlation ρ ∈ [−1, 1]. Diversification helps whenever ρ < 1 and helps most when it is low or negative.
                              • Idiosyncratic risk can be diversified away; systematic risk cannot (floor = ρσ² for equal weights).
                              • Efficient frontier: the least risk for each return. Sharpe ratio = (μp − rf)/σp.
                              • Optimisation magnifies estimation error. Estimated Σ is noisy; use shrinkage and factor models.
                              • Correlations rise in crashes. Diversification is weakest exactly when you most need it.

                              Lesson 10 · Part 3: The maths of risk and return

                              CAPM, beta, alpha and factors

                              In one breath: only the risk you cannot diversify away should earn extra return. That risk is measured by beta, an asset’s sensitivity to the market. Return beyond what beta explains is alpha, the thing every quant hunts. Modern work adds more factors (value, size, momentum), and the Sharpe ratio scores everything in one number. Together this is the language of performance and of every quant interview.

                              About 55 minutesNeeds: Lessons 8 and 9, a little regressionYou will learn: beta, CAPM, alpha, factor models, Sharpe ratio and its noise

                                Beta: your sensitivity to the market

                                Like you’re five

                                The market is the tide. Every boat in the harbour rises and falls with it. A heavy cargo ship barely moves when the tide changes by one metre. A light rowing boat bobs by two. Beta says how much a boat moves when the tide moves by one unit. Beta 1: moves with the tide. Beta 2: moves twice as much. Beta 0.5: moves half as much. A beta of 0 ignores the tide altogether.

                                The real idea

                                Take your stock’s returns and the market’s returns (for example the Hang Seng Index) over many days, and fit a straight line through the scatter of one against the other. The slope is the stock’s beta:

                                ri − rf = α + β (rm − rf) + ε     with    β = Cov(ri, rm)Var(rm)

                                The line splits the stock’s variance in two: the part explained by the market (β²σm², systematic, which you cannot diversify) and the leftover noise ε (idiosyncratic, which you can). The share explained is the regression’s R². Choose a true beta, add company-specific noise, and see what a year of data tells you.

                                In the real world

                                Beta depends on which “market” you choose. For Hong Kong stocks, people often use the Hang Seng Index; for global comparisons, a world index. Betas also change over time and are estimated with error, so quants often shrink them towards 1. High-beta stocks (many technology and small-cap names) fall hard in a market slump. Low-beta stocks (utilities, food retailers) fall less, which is why they are called defensive.

                                CAPM: the price of risk

                                Like you’re five

                                If you want a safe bank deposit, the bank pays you a modest rate. If you want to ride the market’s ups and downs, you should be paid extra for the bumps. And the more your boat bobs with the tide (the higher your beta), the more extra you demand. The risk that is only about your own little boat is not paid, because you could have avoided it for free by owning many boats.

                                The real idea

                                The Capital Asset Pricing Model (Sharpe, Lintner, Mossin, 1960s) says the expected return of any asset is the risk-free rate plus beta times the market’s extra return, the market risk premium:

                                E[ri] = rf + βi × ( E[rm] − rf )

                                Plot expected return against beta and every asset should sit on one straight line, the Security Market Line. The model follows from Lesson 9: if everyone holds the efficient (tangency) portfolio, which must be the whole market, then the risk of any asset is its covariance with the market, which is beta. Try it. Then compare the required return with what you expect a stock to earn. The difference is its alpha. The last line shows a stock’s worth through the Gordon formula from Lesson 6.

                                Quant view

                                CAPM links Lessons 3, 6, 8 and 9. The required return from CAPM is the discount rate in valuation: it is the r in D/(r − g) and in discounted-cash-flow models (Lesson 11), also called the cost of equity. A higher beta means a higher r, a bigger denominator, and a lower price. Empirically, CAPM is only partly right: the line is flatter than predicted (low-beta stocks earn more than the theory says, the “low-beta anomaly”). Still, it is the baseline every other model is measured against.

                                Alpha: return you did not borrow from the market

                                The real idea

                                Alpha (Jensen’s alpha) is the part of a return that beta cannot explain: α = actual excess return − β × market excess return. Positive alpha means skill (or luck, or an unrecognised risk). Beta can be bought almost for free (an index fund); alpha is scarce, and it is what active funds charge high fees for. A manager who just holds high-beta stocks will beat the market in a boom and look like a genius, but has added nothing. Move the market return up and down and compare three funds.

                                Trap

                                Raw returns are the wrong scoreboard. A fund that beats the market with a beta of 1.5 has just taken more risk. Always ask: return per unit of risk (Sharpe ratio), and return left after removing known exposures (alpha).

                                Beyond one factor

                                Like you’re five

                                Two things can make your boat bob: the tide, and also the kind of boat. Small boats behave differently from big ones; old, undervalued boats differ from fashionable, expensive ones. These “kinds” turn out to earn different rewards over time. Each such lasting pattern is called a factor.

                                The real idea

                                Researchers found that stocks with some characteristics earned more than CAPM allows: small companies (size), cheap companies relative to their book value (value), and recent winners (momentum). Fama and French (1993) added size and value to the market factor, and later work added momentum, quality, low volatility and more. A multi-factor model is the same regression with several explanatory returns:

                                ri − rf = α + βmkt Fmkt + βsize Fsize + βval Fval + βmom Fmom + ε

                                Each factor F is the return of a long-short portfolio (for example, “cheap minus expensive”). The big lesson: much of what looks like manager skill under CAPM is just factor exposure, “smart beta”, which can be bought cheaply. Pick a fund and see where its return really comes from. (The factor premia here are illustrative long-run averages, not forecasts.)

                                Quant view

                                This regression is the workhorse of quant research. In practice: (1) every candidate signal is first tested against the standard factors (“is it new, or old value wearing a new hat?”); (2) a strategy’s residual return after factors is its alpha; (3) risk models (Barra, Axioma) describe all stocks’ covariance as Σ = B ΣF Bᵀ + D: loadings B, a small factor covariance ΣF, and a diagonal matrix D of idiosyncratic variances. That is exactly the shrinkage trick from Lesson 9 for estimating a huge covariance matrix with few parameters. And you can already see the link to machine learning: factors are features and alpha is what a model must find beyond them.

                                The Sharpe ratio, and why it is noisy

                                The real idea

                                The Sharpe ratio is excess return per unit of volatility: SR = (μ − rf)/σ. Quoted per year, and for daily data scaled by √252. Rough guide: a broad stock index has a long-run SR of 0.3 to 0.5; a good systematic strategy 1 to 2 after costs; a backtest above 3 is almost always an error or a mirage. A Sharpe ratio is itself an estimate from noisy data. With T years of history its standard error is about √((1 + SR²/2)/T). So with 3 years of data and a true Sharpe of 0.5, one standard error is about 0.6, which means the estimate is typically off by around ±0.6. See the scatter in the demo: each dot is a different 3-year history of the same strategy.

                                In the real world

                                A hedge fund with a 2-year track record and an impressive Sharpe ratio may simply have been lucky, and a strategy with a respectable true Sharpe can easily show a poor one. This is why serious quants demand long data, many independent bets, and out-of-sample tests, and why the multiple-testing problem (trying many strategies and picking the best) is so dangerous. We come back to it in Lessons 16 and 17.

                                Quick check

                                Must-know list

                                • Beta = Cov(ri, rm) / Var(rm) = the regression slope. Measures market sensitivity (systematic risk).
                                • CAPM: E[r] = rf + β × market risk premium. Only systematic risk is rewarded.
                                • The required return from CAPM is the cost of equity, the discount rate for valuation.
                                • Alpha = actual excess return − β × market excess return. Beta is cheap; alpha is scarce.
                                • Factors: size, value, momentum, quality, low volatility. Much “alpha” is factor exposure.
                                • Sharpe ratio = (μ − rf)/σ. Annualise daily by √252. It has a large standard error, about √((1 + SR²/2)/T).
                                • Be suspicious of a high Sharpe ratio from a short or heavily tuned backtest.

                                Company accounts and valuation

                                In one breath: a company reports its life in three documents: the balance sheet (what it has and owes), the income statement (what it earned over a period) and the cash-flow statement (where the cash went). A stock is worth the present value of the cash the company will generate for its owners, which is what a discounted-cash-flow model estimates, and the quick shortcut is to compare multiples such as P/E with similar companies.

                                About 55 minutesNeeds: Lessons 2, 3 and 6You will learn: the three statements, free cash flow, DCF, enterprise value, multiples

                                  The three statements

                                  Like you’re five

                                  Imagine a noodle shop. You could describe it in three different ways. A photo: on 31 December it owns an oven, a stock of noodles and some cash, and it owes the bank some money. That is the balance sheet. A film of the whole year: it took in HK$2 million from customers and spent HK$1.8 million on noodles, staff and rent, so it made HK$0.2 million. That is the income statement. And a diary of the cash drawer: how much money really came in and went out. That is the cash-flow statement. They usually tell different stories, which is why you need all three.

                                  The real idea

                                  Public companies must publish these statements, audited, every year (Hong Kong-listed companies also publish interim results at the half-year). They follow accounting standards (HKFRS in Hong Kong, essentially the same as IFRS; US GAAP in the US).

                                  • Balance sheet: a snapshot at one date. Assets = Liabilities + Equity, exactly the identity from Lesson 2, now for any company. Assets: cash, customers’ unpaid bills (receivables), stock (inventory), machines and buildings. Liabilities: suppliers’ bills, loans, bonds. Equity: what is left for owners.
                                  • Income statement: a movie over a period. Revenue − costs = profit, in steps: revenue, then gross profit (after the direct cost of the goods sold), then operating profit or EBIT (earnings before interest and tax, after running costs), then net profit (after interest and tax). Dividing net profit by the number of shares gives EPS.
                                  • Cash-flow statement: actual cash in and out, split into operating, investing and financing activities.

                                  Play with the income statement of a fictional company, Harbour Noodles Ltd (20 million shares). The bars show where each dollar of revenue goes.

                                  In the real world

                                  The standard profits tax rate for companies in Hong Kong is 16.5% (with a lower rate on the first HK$2 million of profit), which is among the lowest in the developed world. Listed companies announce results on fixed dates, and the announcement itself often moves the price within seconds, because it reveals how profit compared with what the market expected.

                                  Debt, equity and leverage

                                  The real idea

                                  A company finances its assets with debt (which has to be repaid with interest, whatever happens) and equity (owners’ money, paid last). Using debt is called leverage or gearing. As with banks in Lesson 2, leverage multiplies both good years and bad years for the owners. Interest is a fixed cost, so when operating profit is high the owners keep the surplus, and when profit is low the interest still has to be paid. The key ratios:

                                  ROE = net profitequity   |   Interest cover = EBITinterest   |   Debt ratio = debttotal assets

                                  Below is the balance sheet of a company with HK$100 million of assets. Slide the share financed by debt and compare the return on equity in a bad, normal and good year.

                                  Trap

                                  A high ROE is not automatically good. It may just come from heavy borrowing. Always check how a return was produced. Returns on equity that are driven by leverage collapse into losses (and even bankruptcy) when conditions turn. This is why many credit analysts watch interest cover first.

                                  Profit is not cash

                                  Like you’re five

                                  Your shop sells HK$10,000 of noodles to a restaurant, which says “I will pay you next month”. On the income statement you made HK$10,000 of sales today. But nothing is in your cash drawer yet. Meanwhile you bought a new oven for HK$5,000 and that cash is gone. You can have a great profit and an empty drawer. The drawer is what pays the bills.

                                  The real idea

                                  Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash a business generates after paying for everything needed to keep it running and growing, and therefore the cash that could be paid to owners and lenders:

                                  FCF = net profit + depreciation − increase in working capital − capital spending

                                  Depreciation is an accounting cost for the wear of equipment that involves no cash that year, so it is added back. Working capital is cash tied up in stock and unpaid customer bills. Capital expenditure (capex) is cash spent on machines and buildings. FCF is the number that valuation uses, because dividends and future growth come from cash, not from accounting profit.

                                  Valuation by discounted cash flow

                                  Like you’re five

                                  Suppose you could buy a fruit tree. What is it worth? The apples it will give you over all its years, but with each year’s apples counted as a bit less than this year’s, because waiting costs you (Lesson 3). The tree’s price is the sum of all its future apples, each discounted. A company is a tree that produces free cash flow.

                                  The real idea

                                  A discounted cash flow (DCF) model has three steps:

                                  1. Forecast free cash flow for several years (say five), using a growth rate.
                                  2. Terminal value: lump together everything after the forecast period with the Gordon formula from Lesson 6, TV = FCF5(1 + g)/(r − g), using a modest long-run growth rate g (never above the economy’s growth).
                                  3. Discount everything at the discount rate r (from CAPM in Lesson 10, blended with the cost of debt it is called WACC) and add up. This is the enterprise value (EV), the value of the whole business to all providers of capital.
                                  Equity value = EV − net debt   (net debt = debt − cash)   ⇒   value per share = equity value ÷ shares

                                  Change the assumptions and watch the answer swing.

                                  Trap

                                  The model feels precise and is not. Look at the sensitivity table: moving the discount rate by one point or the terminal growth by one point changes the value by a third or more. Most of the value sits in the terminal value, which is the part nobody can forecast. Treat a DCF as a way to organise your assumptions, not as a measurement of the truth. “Garbage in, garbage out.”

                                  Quant view

                                  Enterprise value equals equity value plus net debt, and a company’s EV/EBITDA multiple can be compared across companies with different debt levels, which P/E cannot. Quants rarely build a DCF for each stock. Instead they use ratios from the accounts as features (earnings yield E/P, book-to-market, cash flow yield, profitability, accruals), which are exactly the “value” and “quality” factors from Lesson 10. One warning that matters enormously for backtests: accounts for a year ending in December are only published months later, and are sometimes restated. You must use the data as it was known on each date (“point-in-time” data), or you will leak the future into your model.

                                  Valuation by multiples

                                  Like you’re five

                                  You want to price your friend’s house. You look at what similar houses next door sold for per square metre. If the neighbours’ houses sold for HK$150,000 per square metre, yours is probably worth about that. For companies, the “per square metre” is a multiple: how many dollars investors pay for one dollar of earnings, or of sales, or of book value.

                                  The real idea

                                  The most common multiple is P/E = price ÷ EPS. Others: EV/EBITDA, P/B (price ÷ book value of equity), and P/S (price ÷ sales). Relative valuation compares a company’s multiple with those of comparable companies and assumes the market has priced them roughly correctly. The catch is who is comparable. A bank with 3% growth and a software company growing 20% a year should not trade on the same multiple. Growth is part of the answer: from the Gordon formula, P/E ≈ payout ÷ (r − g), so higher growth means a higher P/E. The chart shows eight fictional peers. Regress P/E on growth, then ask what P/E a company with a given growth rate deserves.

                                  Why this matters to a trader

                                  In the real world

                                  Fundamental analysts build the models above one company at a time. Quant funds turn the same accounts into signals across thousands of stocks at once: buy the cheap and profitable, avoid the expensive and deteriorating. Both rely on the same truth: a share is a claim on cash flows, and a price that departs from what the cash flows justify is an opportunity if your estimate is better than the market’s. That “if” is the subject of Lesson 15.

                                  Quick check

                                  Must-know list

                                  • Balance sheet: Assets = Liabilities + Equity (a snapshot). Income statement: revenue − costs = profit (a period). Cash flow: real cash in and out.
                                  • Income steps: revenue → gross profit → EBIT → net profit → EPS.
                                  • Leverage multiplies ROE in good years and bad years. Check interest cover = EBIT ÷ interest.
                                  • Free cash flow = net profit + depreciation − increase in working capital − capex. Profit ≠ cash.
                                  • DCF: discount forecast FCF and a terminal value TV = FCF(1+g)/(r − g). Value is extremely sensitive to r and g.
                                  • EV = equity value + net debt. Equity value per share = (EV − net debt) ÷ shares.
                                  • Multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/B) compare companies, but only comparable ones. Growth matters: P/E ≈ payout ÷ (r − g).
                                  • For backtests, use point-in-time accounting data. Otherwise you leak the future.

                                  Forwards and futures

                                  In one breath: a derivative is a contract whose value comes from something else: a stock, an index, a currency, a commodity. A forward or future locks in a price today for a trade that happens later. Its fair price is not a forecast but the spot price plus the cost of waiting (interest minus yield), or an arbitrageur could make risk-free money. Futures are mostly used to hedge risk, and they give leverage, which cuts both ways.

                                  About 50 minutesNeeds: Lessons 3 and 9You will learn: payoffs, margin, cost of carry, arbitrage, hedging

                                    What is a derivative?

                                    Like you’re five

                                    You tell the baker today: “In three months I will buy 100 loaves from you at HK$10 each, whatever the price of flour is then.” Nothing happens now. In three months, the deal happens at HK$10. The contract between you has a value of its own that changes as flour gets pricier or cheaper. That contract is a derivative: it derives its value from another price.

                                    The real idea

                                    A derivative is a financial contract whose payoff depends on the price of an underlying asset (a stock, index, currency, commodity, interest rate, even the weather). The main families are forwards and futures (this lesson), options (Lessons 13 and 14) and swaps. People use them for three reasons:

                                    • Hedging: reducing a risk you already have (an airline fixing its fuel cost).
                                    • Speculation: taking a leveraged bet on a price move.
                                    • Arbitrage: locking in a risk-free profit when two prices are inconsistent.

                                    Derivatives are zero-sum: every dollar one side gains, the other loses, excluding costs. And their size is described by the notional value, the value of the underlying that the contract controls, which is usually many times the cash needed to open it.

                                    Forwards: a price agreed today

                                    The real idea

                                    A forward contract is an agreement between two parties to trade an asset at a fixed delivery price K on a future date T. The buyer is long the forward, the seller is short. No money changes hands at the start. At maturity, if the market (spot) price is ST:

                                    long payoff = ST − K     short payoff = K − ST

                                    The long side wins if the price rose above K; the short side wins if it fell. Drag the final price.

                                    Quant view

                                    The payoff is linear in ST, with slope +1 for the long and −1 for the short. A linear payoff is the simplest building block, and the next lesson’s options will have bent payoffs. The forward price K that makes the contract worth zero when signed is not a prediction of the future spot price: it is determined by arbitrage, as shown below.

                                    Futures, margin and leverage

                                    Like you’re five

                                    A forward is a promise on a handshake: what if your friend does not pay when the day comes? A futures contract fixes this by using a referee. Every evening, the referee checks who is winning and moves the money immediately from the loser’s deposit to the winner’s. If your deposit runs low, the referee says “top it up, or you are out”. So nobody can build up a huge unpaid debt.

                                    The real idea

                                    A futures contract is a standardised forward traded on an exchange. Differences from a forward:

                                    • Standard contracts: set size, set expiry dates, so the contract is easy to trade.
                                    • Clearing house: it stands between buyer and seller, so each side only faces the clearing house, which removes counterparty risk.
                                    • Margin: you post an initial margin, a small deposit (often 5 to 15% of the notional value), into your account.
                                    • Daily settlement (mark-to-market): gains and losses are credited or debited to the margin account every day. If the balance falls below the maintenance margin, you get a margin call and must top up to the initial level or the position is closed.

                                    The result is leverage. With 10% margin, a 1% move in the index changes your equity by 10%. The demo uses a Hang Seng Index future (multiplier HK$50 per index point, so at 20,000 points one contract controls HK$1,000,000). Run a month of trading.

                                    Trap

                                    Leverage is not extra profit, it is extra variance. The same move that doubles your margin can wipe it out, and you can be forced to close at the worst moment (a margin call is a forced sale). Many blow-ups follow this pattern, most famously the collapse of Barings Bank in 1995 from unauthorised Nikkei futures positions. Sizing positions so that you survive a bad run is a core quant skill (Lesson 16).

                                    In the real world

                                    Popular contracts: Hang Seng Index futures and the smaller Mini-HSI contract on HKEX (multipliers HK$50 and HK$10), the E-mini S&P 500 on CME (US$50 per point), gold, oil, US Treasury bonds and currency futures. Each expires on a set date; before expiry, traders who want to keep their position roll it into the next contract. Margins are set by the exchange and raised in volatile times, so check the current figures before trading anything.

                                    The fair forward price: cost of carry

                                    Like you’re five

                                    You want a bag of rice in one year. Plan A: sign a forward today. Plan B: buy the bag today, store it for a year, and use it then. Plan B costs you the bag’s price now (money you could have earned interest on), minus anything the rice earns while you wait. For the two plans to be equally good, the forward price must equal the spot price plus the cost of waiting. If not, there is free money, and clever people take it until the gap closes.

                                    The real idea

                                    Replicating a forward by buying the asset now and holding it gives the cost-of-carry formula. With a risk-free rate r and the asset paying a continuous yield q (dividends for a stock index, a convenience benefit for a commodity, minus storage costs):

                                    F = S · e(r − q)T

                                    If the market futures price differs from this, there is an arbitrage. If Fmarket is too high: borrow S at r, buy the asset, sell the future (cash-and-carry); at T deliver the asset, repay the loan and keep the difference. If too low: do the reverse. Notice that r and q are the only inputs: nobody’s opinion about the future price appears. When r > q the futures price rises with maturity (contango); when q > r it falls (backwardation). Change the inputs and also the market price.

                                    Quant view

                                    This is your first no-arbitrage pricing argument, and the template for everything in derivatives: find a portfolio of simpler assets that gives the same payoff, then the derivative must have the same price. It needs no model of the future price, no probabilities and no risk preferences. Practical caveats: transaction costs, borrowing spreads and short-selling limits create a no-arbitrage band around the fair value, and for storable commodities the “convenience yield” is itself estimated from the futures curve. The gap between spot and futures is the basis, and it converges to zero at expiry.

                                    Hedging with futures

                                    Like you’re five

                                    You own a boat. A storm might sink the whole harbour’s boats at once. You buy insurance by agreeing that if the harbour’s boats lose value, someone pays you. Now a storm does not hurt you much, but a sunny year does not help you much either. Hedging trades away both the bad luck and the good luck.

                                    The real idea

                                    Suppose you own HK$12 million of stocks with a beta of 1.2 (Lesson 10) and fear a market drop, but do not want to sell. You can short index futures. The number of contracts is the hedge ratio:

                                    N = β × portfolio valuefutures notional per contract

                                    If the market falls, your stocks lose and your short futures gain. What remains is only the part of your return that did not come from the market, your stock-picking. That is the foundation of market-neutral and long-short equity strategies. Try a falling and a rising market.

                                    Think about it

                                    A hedge removes market risk, and with it the market’s reward (the risk premium from Lesson 10). So why do funds do it? Because if they believe they have genuine alpha, hedging lets them harvest alpha without being at the mercy of the market, and charge for it. If the alpha is not real, the hedged fund just earns roughly the risk-free rate, minus costs.

                                    In the real world

                                    Hedging is also how everyday businesses use futures: an airline fixes its fuel costs, a miner locks in the price of gold, an exporter fixes the exchange rate. Speculators take the other side. Their willingness to take risk is what makes hedging cheap. Arbitrageurs keep the futures price in line with spot.

                                    Quick check

                                    Must-know list

                                    • A derivative’s value derives from an underlying. Uses: hedge, speculate, arbitrage. Zero-sum.
                                    • Forward: agreed price K for later. Long payoff ST − K; short payoff K − ST. No cash at the start.
                                    • Futures: standardised, exchange-traded, cleared; margin and daily settlement; margin call below maintenance.
                                    • Leverage = notional ÷ margin. It magnifies gains and losses.
                                    • Cost of carry: F = Se(r − q)T. Enforced by arbitrage, independent of forecasts.
                                    • Contango (futures above spot, r > q) and backwardation. The basis goes to zero at expiry.
                                    • Hedge ratio N = β × portfolio value ÷ contract notional. What remains after hedging is the stock-specific part, the alpha.

                                    Options from scratch

                                    In one breath: an option gives you the right but not the obligation to buy (a call) or sell (a put) something at a fixed price. You pay a premium for that right. Because you can walk away, your loss is capped but your gain is not, and that bent, max(·, 0) shaped payoff is what makes options special: they let you build insurance, cheap leveraged bets, and bets on volatility itself.

                                    About 55 minutesNeeds: Lessons 3 and 12You will learn: calls, puts, payoffs, spreads, straddles, put-call parity, option leverage

                                      The right, but not the obligation

                                      Like you’re five

                                      You see a flat you like for HK$5 million and the price is rising. You pay the owner HK$100,000 today for a promise: “for the next three months, you may buy it from me for HK$5 million.” If prices jump to HK$6 million, you use your right and gain HK$1 million (minus your HK$100,000 fee). If prices fall, you simply walk away and lose only the fee. With a forward from Lesson 12 you would be forced to buy; here you are not. That freedom costs money, and the fee is called the premium.

                                      The real idea

                                      An option is a contract with these terms:

                                      • Call: the right to buy the underlying at the strike price K. Put: the right to sell at K.
                                      • Expiry T: the date the right ends. A European option can only be used at expiry; an American option any time before it.
                                      • Premium: the price you pay today for the option. The seller (writer) receives it, and takes on the obligation if you use your right.

                                      At expiry, if the stock price is S:

                                      call payoff = max(S − K, 0)     put payoff = max(K − S, 0)     profit = payoff − premium

                                      You only exercise when it helps. Pick a position and drag the final price. (Numbers are per share; a real contract covers many shares.)

                                      Trap

                                      The two sides are not symmetric. A buyer can lose only the premium. A seller of a call can lose without limit (the stock can rise forever), and a seller of a put can lose nearly the whole strike. Selling options collects small premiums most of the time and occasionally takes a huge loss: “picking up pennies in front of a steamroller”. Many traders have been wiped out this way.

                                      In the real world

                                      Each contract covers a fixed number of shares set by the exchange (100 for most US stocks). Index options such as Hang Seng Index options on HKEX use a multiplier of HK$50 per point and are cash-settled: no shares change hands, you just receive the payoff in cash. Expiry dates are set by the exchange, typically monthly, with some weekly ones. Always check the contract specifications before trading.

                                      In, at and out of the money

                                      The real idea

                                      An option is in the money (ITM) if exercising it right now would pay something, at the money (ATM) if S ≈ K, and out of the money (OTM) otherwise. The premium has two parts:

                                      premium = intrinsic value + time value

                                      Intrinsic value is what you would get if you exercised now (the payoff formula with today’s S). Time value is the extra you pay for the chance that things get better before expiry. It is biggest at the money, grows with volatility and with time left, and melts to zero at expiry (“time decay”). Here is a table for a stock trading at HK$105.

                                      OptionStatusIntrinsic value
                                      Call, K = 100in the money5
                                      Call, K = 105at the money0
                                      Call, K = 110out of the money0
                                      Put, K = 110in the money5
                                      Put, K = 100out of the money0

                                      An OTM option costs only time value, which is why it is cheap, and why it usually expires worthless.

                                      Strategies: building with options

                                      Like you’re five

                                      Options are Lego bricks. One brick is a bet. Snap two or three together and you can build a roof over your stock (a floor under your losses), a bet that nothing much will happen, or a bet that something big will happen, without caring which way. You choose the shape of your profit and loss, and you pay for the shape you want.

                                      The real idea

                                      You can combine stock and options (the legs), and the profit curve is just the sum of the legs’ curves. The classic shapes:

                                      • Covered call: own the stock, sell a call above. Earn the premium, give up big gains.
                                      • Protective put: own the stock, buy a put below. Pay a premium, your loss is floored: insurance.
                                      • Collar: both of the above; the call’s premium pays for the put.
                                      • Bull call spread: buy a call, sell a higher call. Cheaper than a plain call, but profit is capped.
                                      • Straddle / strangle: buy a call and a put. You win if the stock moves a lot in either direction.
                                      • Butterfly: buy a low call, sell two middle calls, buy a high call. You win if the stock stays near the middle.

                                      Pick one, move the strike spacing, and check the cost, the best case and the worst case. The premiums come from a pricing model that Lesson 14 explains; here just treat them as fair market prices.

                                      Quant view

                                      Here is a connection you will like. The payoff of a call is max(S − K, 0), which is exactly the ReLU activation from neural networks. Stacking calls with different strikes gives piecewise-linear payoffs, so with enough calls you can build any payoff shape, just as a ReLU network can approximate any function. A butterfly is a triangular “hat” function, like a basis function in a spline. This “options as basis functions” idea is why a strip of option prices at every strike reveals the market’s whole view of the future price distribution (Lesson 14).

                                      In the real world

                                      Pension funds and asset managers buy puts to protect portfolios. Income funds sell covered calls to collect premium. Traders use straddles around events such as earnings announcements, betting that the move will be bigger than the options imply. Each of these is a trade-off, not free money: the premium paid or the upside given up is the price of the shape.

                                      Put-call parity

                                      Like you’re five

                                      Two ways to be sure of having at least HK$100 in a year while also keeping any gain above 100 on a stock. Way 1: put HK$100 in a safe account and also buy a call struck at 100 (the call gives you the upside). Way 2: buy the stock and also buy a put struck at 100 (the put protects you below 100). On the last day, both give you exactly the same amount of money, whatever the stock did. So they must cost the same today. If they did not, you would buy the cheap one, sell the expensive one and pocket the difference.

                                      The real idea

                                      Compare two portfolios at expiry:

                                      • A: one call plus cash worth K at expiry (that is Ke−rT today). Worth max(S − K, 0) + K = max(S, K).
                                      • B: one put plus one share. Worth max(K − S, 0) + S = max(S, K).

                                      Same payoff in every scenario, so same price today:

                                      C − P = S − Ke−rT

                                      This is put-call parity (for European options on a stock paying no dividends). Move the strike and the final price: the two payoffs always coincide. Then make the put “mispriced” and see the arbitrage.

                                      Quant view

                                      Parity is a model-free no-arbitrage relation, like cost of carry in Lesson 12: it uses no probabilities and no volatility. It tells you that a call and a put are really the same bet once you add or subtract the stock and a loan, so you only have to understand one of them (this halves the work in Lesson 14). It also lets you create synthetic positions: a long call plus short put at the same strike behaves exactly like a forward, since C − P = S − Ke−rT. With dividends the formula changes slightly (replace S by Se−qT), and for American options parity becomes an inequality.

                                      Options as leverage, and why most expire worthless

                                      Like you’re five

                                      HK$1,000 buys you 10 shares of a HK$100 stock. Or the same HK$1,000 might buy you lottery-like tickets that pay out a lot if the stock jumps, but are worth nothing if it does not. If the stock goes up 10%, your shares make 10%, while your tickets might make several times your money. If it goes down or just sits still, the tickets are worth zero and the shares barely changed. Tickets: big upside, high chance of losing everything.

                                      The real idea

                                      A call costs a small fraction of the stock, so the same cash controls far more stock: option leverage. Unlike futures (Lesson 12), you cannot lose more than the premium. But you lose all of it often. The chart compares the return on buying a stock with the return on spending the same cash on a call. Change the strike, the time to expiry and the volatility, then slide the final move.

                                      Trap

                                      Leverage and a high hit rate are not the same. Out-of-the-money options are a series of small, likely losses and rare big wins. Even when the average bet is fair, an option buyer who bets all of their cash repeatedly tends to go broke. The mirror image is the seller, who wins often and loses big. Position size (Lesson 16) matters more than the instrument.

                                      Think about it

                                      If a call’s value were just “expected stock price minus strike”, a very volatile stock would be worth the same as a calm one with the same average price. But the demo shows the call’s payoff is bent: when the stock rises you gain all of it, when it falls you lose only the premium. So higher volatility makes an option more valuable. Options are, in effect, a way to buy or sell volatility. How much more valuable? That is exactly the question Black, Scholes and Merton answered, and the next lesson.

                                      Quick check

                                      Must-know list

                                      • Call = right to buy at K; put = right to sell at K. Buyer pays a premium; seller (writer) takes the obligation.
                                      • Payoffs at expiry: call max(S − K, 0), put max(K − S, 0). Profit = payoff − premium (for the buyer).
                                      • Buyer’s loss is capped at the premium. A naked call seller’s loss is unlimited.
                                      • ITM / ATM / OTM. Premium = intrinsic value + time value; time value decays to zero at expiry.
                                      • Strategies add legs: covered call, protective put (insurance), collar, spreads, straddle (bet on volatility), butterfly (bet on calm).
                                      • A call’s payoff is a ReLU; combinations of calls can build any piecewise-linear payoff.
                                      • Put-call parity: C − P = S − Ke−rT. A call plus a short put at the same strike is a synthetic forward.
                                      • Options give leverage without margin calls, but most OTM options expire worthless. More volatility means a higher option price.

                                      Pricing options: binomial trees, Black–Scholes and the Greeks

                                      In one breath: an option can be copied by trading the stock and a loan, so its price is the cost of that copy, and nobody’s opinion about where the stock is going matters. Take the copying in tiny steps and you get the Black–Scholes formula, with volatility as the only unknown. The Greeks measure how the price reacts to everything, a delta hedge turns an option into a pure bet on volatility, and implied volatility is the market’s price of that bet.

                                      About 70 minutesNeeds: Lessons 8, 12 and 13, a little calculusYou will learn: replication, risk-neutral pricing, Black–Scholes, Greeks, delta hedging, implied volatility, the smile

                                        Pricing an option by copying it

                                        Like you’re five

                                        A magic coin decides whether a stock goes up to HK$120 or down to HK$90 next year. A call at HK$100 pays HK$20 if up, HK$0 if down. Here is the trick: you can build the same payoff with a recipe, “hold some shares and borrow some money”. If two things pay exactly the same in every possible future, they must cost the same today, otherwise you could buy the cheap one, sell the expensive one and keep the difference with no risk. So the call’s price is simply the price of the recipe. We never needed to know how likely the up move is.

                                        The real idea

                                        Let the stock be S0 now and either Su or Sd a year later, and let the option pay fu or fd. Find the replicating portfolio: Δ shares plus an amount B in the bank (negative means borrowing) so that it pays the same in both cases:

                                        Δ = fu − fdSu − Sd     price = ΔS0 + B = e−rT [ q fu + (1 − q) fd ]     q = erT − du − d

                                        Here u = Su/S0 and d = Sd/S0. The number q acts like a probability, but it is not the real-world chance of an up move: it is the weight that makes the stock’s average return equal the risk-free rate. It is called the risk-neutral probability. Change “your probability” in the demo and watch the price not move.

                                        Quant view

                                        Pricing as discounted expectation under q (risk-neutral pricing) is the central trick of derivatives. It looks like we ignored the probability of up moves, and that is exactly right: the probability is already reflected in the stock’s price today. If the stock is likely to rise, its price is higher now, so the option’s price relative to the stock is the same. The recipe also needs d < erT < u, otherwise the stock itself would be an arbitrage against the bank (Lesson 12).

                                        Many steps, and a smooth curve appears

                                        Like you’re five

                                        One coin flip is crude. So flip a coin every month instead, then every week, then every day. The stock now moves along a tree with many branches. The price of the option is found by starting at expiry (where the payoff is known), and stepping back one level at a time using the recipe from before. As you chop time into thinner slices the price settles to one number.

                                        The real idea

                                        The Cox–Ross–Rubinstein (CRR) binomial tree divides time into n steps of length Δt. At each step the stock multiplies by u = eσ√Δt or by d = 1/u. Work backwards from the payoffs at the last level. For an American option you can also exercise early, so at each node take the larger of “continue” and “exercise now”. This is a tiny piece of dynamic programming, and the only way to price early exercise in a simple model. As n grows the answer converges to the Black–Scholes price. Look at the convergence, and then switch to the American put.

                                        The Black–Scholes formula

                                        The real idea

                                        Letting the steps shrink to nothing gives the Black–Scholes–Merton formula (1973; Scholes and Merton received the 1997 Nobel Prize in economics, and Fischer Black had died in 1995). It assumes the stock follows the geometric random walk of Lesson 8 with constant volatility σ, trading is continuous and free, and the interest rate r is constant. For a European call on a stock paying no dividends:

                                        C = S Φ(d1) − K e−rT Φ(d2)     d1 = ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)Tσ√T     d2 = d1 − σ√T

                                        Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Reading the formula: the call is “buy a share (weighted by Φ(d1)) financed by borrowing the strike (weighted by Φ(d2))”, and Φ(d2) is the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money. For a put, P = Ke−rTΦ(−d2) − SΦ(−d1), which also follows from put-call parity. Five inputs: S, K, T, r (all observable) and σ (not observable). That one unknown is why options are about volatility. Experiment below.

                                        Quant view

                                        Where the formula comes from, in three lines. With dS = μS dt + σS dW and an option value V(S, t), Itô’s lemma gives dV = (Vt + μSVS + ½σ²S²VSS)dt + σSVS dW. The portfolio V − ΔS with Δ = VS has no dW term, so it is riskless and must earn r. The result is the Black–Scholes PDE:

                                        Vt + ½σ²S²VSS + rS VS − rV = 0,    V(S, T) = payoff

                                        The stock’s expected return μ has vanished, as in the tree. The PDE is a heat equation in disguise, and its solution equals V = e−rT EQ[payoff], where under Q the stock grows at r. Monte Carlo simulation of this expectation is how banks price exotic options that have no formula.

                                        Trap

                                        The model is a language, not the truth. Real volatility is not constant, prices jump, returns have fat tails (Lesson 8) and hedging is neither continuous nor free. Traders therefore do not use Black–Scholes to find “the” price. They use it to translate between a price and a volatility, as in the last section of this lesson.

                                        The Greeks: how the price reacts

                                        Like you’re five

                                        An option’s price is like a car’s fuel use: it depends on many things at once. The Greeks are the dashboard dials. One dial says how much the price changes if the stock moves by HK$1. Another says how much it changes each day just because time passes. Another says what a one-point change in volatility does. Traders read these dials all day, because they tell them what they are really exposed to.

                                        The real idea

                                        Each Greek is a partial derivative of the option price V with respect to one input, so you already know the idea from multivariable calculus:

                                        • Delta Δ = ∂V/∂S. Price change per HK$1 stock move; the number of shares that hedges one option. For a call between 0 and 1 (about 0.5 at the money, near 1 deep in the money); for a put between −1 and 0.
                                        • Gamma Γ = ∂²V/∂S². How fast delta itself changes. High near the strike and close to expiry.
                                        • Theta Θ = ∂V/∂t. Value lost per day with time passing (time decay). Usually negative for an option buyer.
                                        • Vega = ∂V/∂σ. Price change per rise in volatility. Largest at the money and for long maturities (Vega is not a Greek letter, but the name stuck).
                                        • Rho = ∂V/∂r. Sensitivity to interest rates. Usually small.

                                        Pick a Greek and compare your option with the same option one month from expiry. Notice how near expiry, delta becomes a cliff and gamma spikes.

                                        Quant view

                                        For small moves the price changes by a Taylor expansion, and the Greeks are its coefficients: ΔV ≈ Δ·ΔS + ½Γ·(ΔS)² + Θ·Δt + vega·Δσ. A trading desk’s “risk report” is just these Greeks summed over thousands of positions, so a portfolio can be made delta-neutral (no first-order exposure to the stock), gamma-neutral and so on. A useful identity from the PDE: for a delta-hedged position Θ + ½σ²S²Γ = rV − rSΔ, so time decay and gamma are two sides of the same coin.

                                        Delta hedging: you are really trading volatility

                                        Like you’re five

                                        You sell someone an option. If the stock shoots up you could lose a lot. So you also buy some shares, and keep adjusting how many you hold as the stock moves, so that you hardly care which way it goes. What is left? You collected a premium for the option, and you pay for the shuffling. If the stock jiggles more than the price assumed, the shuffling costs you more than you were paid, and you lose. If it jiggles less, you win. So you are not betting on up or down, you are betting on how much it jiggles.

                                        The real idea

                                        Delta hedging: the seller of a call holds Δ shares and re-balances as Δ changes. Per small time step, the hedged seller’s profit is about ½ΓS²(σimplied² − σrealised²)Δt. So if you sell options at an implied volatility above the volatility that then actually occurs, you profit; below it, you lose. Whether the stock goes up or down does not enter. The demo simulates 300 hedged sellers. Try hedging never, monthly, weekly and daily, and set the realised volatility above, equal to and below the implied one.

                                        In the real world

                                        This is the business of option market makers and volatility funds: sell options when implied volatility looks too high compared with what they expect to be realised, hedge the direction away, and collect the difference (the volatility risk premium, which tends to be positive on average for equity indices, because investors pay up for insurance). The risk is a sudden jump, since no hedge can keep up with a gap, and the strategy has the shape of the “steamroller” trade from Lesson 13.

                                        Implied volatility and the smile

                                        Like you’re five

                                        You see the price tag on an option in the shop. The formula needs “how jumpy is the stock” as a dial, and the price tag tells you where the dial must be set for the formula to match it. That reading is the implied volatility: the market’s own opinion about how jumpy the stock will be, read off from option prices.

                                        The real idea

                                        The Black–Scholes price rises steadily with σ, so for any observed market price there is exactly one σ that reproduces it, found by root-finding: Newton’s method on f(σ) = BS(σ) − market price, whose derivative is vega. That σ is the implied volatility (IV). Option traders quote prices in IV, as bond traders quote yields (Lesson 5): it is a better way to compare options with different strikes and expiries. Drag the market price.

                                        The smile

                                        If Black–Scholes were true, every option on the same stock and expiry would have the same IV. In reality it is not flat. For equity indices the IV is much higher for low strikes (out-of-the-money puts) than for high strikes: the volatility skew or smile. It appeared after the 1987 crash, when people began to pay a lot for crash insurance, and it has never gone away. It says that the market assigns more probability to large falls than a normal curve does (fat tails, Lesson 8), and that investors pay for protection.

                                        In the real world

                                        The market’s overall IV is published as a single index: the VIX for the US S&P 500 and the VHSI for the Hang Seng Index. They are computed from a whole strip of option prices and called the “fear gauge”: they jump in sell-offs. For a quant, the implied-volatility surface (IV across strikes and expiries) is a rich dataset: it is used for features, for risk and for volatility-trading strategies.

                                        Quick check

                                        Must-know list

                                        • An option is replicated by Δ shares plus a loan, so price = cost of the replica. Real-world probabilities do not matter.
                                        • Risk-neutral pricing: price = e−rT × expected payoff, where the stock grows at r. q = (erT − d)/(u − d) in the tree.
                                        • The binomial tree works backwards from expiry (dynamic programming), handles American options, and converges to Black–Scholes as steps grow.
                                        • Black–Scholes: C = SΦ(d1) − Ke−rTΦ(d2). Inputs S, K, T, r, σ. The stock’s expected return does not appear.
                                        • Greeks: Δ (hedge ratio), Γ (how fast Δ changes), Θ (time decay), vega (volatility), ρ (rates).
                                        • A delta-hedged seller earns ½ΓS²(σimp² − σreal²)Δt: options are a bet on volatility.
                                        • Implied volatility = the σ that matches the market price (solve by Newton’s method). The smile or skew shows that the model’s flat σ is wrong, especially for crash risk.

                                        Efficient markets, and why an edge is rare

                                        In one breath: thousands of smart, well-funded people compete to price every asset correctly, so prices already contain most of what is publicly known. That makes consistent outperformance rare, small and fragile. Luck looks like skill, patterns appear in noise, and published anomalies fade. Yet markets are not perfectly efficient (people are biased and arbitrage is costly), and a small edge repeated across many independent bets can still be worth a lot. Knowing exactly how hard the problem is is the most useful thing a new quant can learn.

                                        About 55 minutesNeeds: Lessons 8, 10 and 11You will learn: the efficient market hypothesis, testing for predictability, luck versus skill, anomalies, behavioural finance, the fundamental law

                                          Can anyone beat the market?

                                          Like you’re five

                                          A big jar is full of jellybeans. Hundreds of children each guess how many. Nobody is very accurate, but the average of all the guesses is surprisingly close to the truth, because the individual mistakes go in all directions and cancel out. Now you try to beat that average: you would need to be better than the whole crowd put together. A stock market is such a crowd, guessing what each company is worth, with real money riding on every guess. The price you see is the crowd’s average guess.

                                          The real idea

                                          This is the heart of the efficient market hypothesis (EMH). When many people make independent estimates with independent errors, the average error shrinks like 1/√N (Lesson 9: diversification of mistakes). So the crowd average beats most individuals. The catch is the word independent. If everyone makes the same mistake (herding, a shared model, a shared story), the crowd is no better than one person. Try both.

                                          Quant view

                                          The demo is the variance formula from Lesson 9 again: the average of N estimates with error variance σ² and pairwise correlation ρ has variance σ²(ρ + (1 − ρ)/N). As N grows it falls to σ²ρ, not zero. Prices are efficient to the degree that mistakes are uncorrelated, and a market edge comes from finding the places where mistakes are correlated (everyone anchors on the same wrong thing) or where the crowd is missing.

                                          Three forms of efficiency

                                          The real idea

                                          Eugene Fama formalised the idea in 1970 (he shared the 2013 Nobel Prize with Robert Shiller, who argued the opposite, and Lars Peter Hansen). Paul Samuelson had shown in 1965 that in a market where information is quickly used, price changes become unpredictable, the random walk of Lesson 8. The hypothesis comes in three strengths, each stating which information is already in the price:

                                          FormPrices already reflect…What it rules out
                                          WeakAll past prices and volumesMaking money from chart patterns alone (technical analysis)
                                          Semi-strongAll public information (news, accounts)Making money from reading reports and headlines faster than others
                                          StrongEverything, including private (inside) informationEven insiders profiting (this form is false, and insider trading is illegal for that reason)

                                          “Efficient” does not mean the price is always right. It means it is unbiased: you cannot predict the error’s sign, and so cannot reliably profit from it after costs and risk.

                                          Think about it

                                          If markets were perfectly efficient, nobody would have a reason to spend money analysing companies, since the price would already be right. But then prices would stop being right, since nobody does the analysing. This is the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox (1980). The resolution: markets are efficient only enough that the profit from research roughly equals its cost. The small edge that remains is the payment for finding information and for bearing risk. It is a competitive wage, not free money.

                                          In the real world

                                          One reason the hypothesis holds up well: the best tests are what active funds actually achieve. The S&P SPIVA scorecards have shown for many years that most actively managed funds trail their benchmark index over ten years or longer, once fees are included. And yet there are clear cases where the same asset trades at two prices: many Chinese companies are listed in both Shanghai (A-shares) and Hong Kong (H-shares), and the A-share price is often at a notable premium, tracked by the Hang Seng Stock Connect China AH Premium Index. Capital controls and investor restrictions are the “limit to arbitrage” that lets such gaps persist (see below).

                                          Testing for predictability

                                          Like you’re five

                                          Flip a fair coin 100 times and you will almost surely see a run of five heads somewhere. Nothing is wrong with the coin: streaks are what randomness looks like. People are terrible at telling real patterns from lucky ones. So we do not trust our eyes, we measure, and we ask how big a pattern would need to be to stand out from chance.

                                          The real idea

                                          The simplest test of weak-form efficiency is autocorrelation: does today’s return correlate with yesterday’s, or with the return k days ago? For N independent returns, the sample autocorrelation is roughly normal with standard deviation 1/√N, so about 95% of the time it lies within ±1.96/√N (the dashed band). Two consequences:

                                          • With 20 lags tested at the 95% level you expect one false alarm even when there is nothing there. This is the multiple-testing problem.
                                          • A real but tiny autocorrelation (say 0.05) is invisible unless you have thousands of days of data. A small edge needs a lot of evidence.

                                          Simulate returns with a hidden autocorrelation and see what a sample reveals.

                                          Trap

                                          If you look at enough patterns, some will “work” in your data. Researchers call it data mining or p-hacking, and it is the most common way a promising signal turns out to be nothing. The defence is to decide what to test before looking, to count every test you try, and to demand much stronger evidence (a higher t-statistic) the more things you tried. Lessons 16 and 17 build this into a proper process.

                                          Luck looks like skill

                                          Like you’re five

                                          A thousand children each flip a coin. Everyone who flips tails sits down. Flip again, half sit down. After ten rounds, about one child is still standing, having flipped ten heads in a row. Is that child a coin-flipping genius? No, we simply started with a thousand. But the newspaper only interviews the child left standing.

                                          The real idea

                                          Replace “coin flip” with “beat the market this year”, and children with fund managers. Even if every manager were pure luck, with N managers you expect about N/2Y of them to beat the market Y years in a row. Any individual’s record looks impressive, but the probability that someone has it is high. Add survivorship bias (failed funds close and vanish from the record) and the surviving record looks even better than reality. Simulate it.

                                          In the real world

                                          This is why a track record of only a few years is weak evidence (Lesson 10: the Sharpe ratio of a short history is very noisy), and why serious investors ask: how many managers or strategies did you start with, and what happened to those not shown? The same logic applies to you: if you test 100 strategies on the same data and show only the best one, the track record is not a result, it is a selection.

                                          Anomalies, and what happens to them

                                          The real idea

                                          An anomaly is a pattern in average returns that the simple models of Lesson 10 do not explain. Famous ones:

                                          AnomalyPatternPossible explanation
                                          MomentumPast 3 to 12 month winners keep beating losers for a while (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993)Slow reaction to news; herding; crashes when it reverses
                                          ValueCheap stocks (low price relative to book value or earnings) outperform on averageCompensation for risk, or overreaction to bad news
                                          SizeSmall companies earned more than large ones historicallyIlliquidity and risk; weaker in recent decades
                                          Post-earnings driftPrices keep drifting in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeksUnder-reaction; costly arbitrage
                                          Low volatilityCalm stocks earn about as much as wild ones (so more per unit of risk)Investors overpay for lottery-like stocks; leverage limits

                                          Three lessons from the research. (1) Anomalies decay: McLean and Pontiff (2016) found that predictability falls substantially after publication (their estimate: about 58% lower), as traders find and trade it away. (2) Too many factors: researchers have published hundreds of factors, and Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) argue that with this many tests a new factor should clear a t-statistic of about 3, not 2. (3) Risk or mispricing? The same pattern can be a reward for bearing risk (a fair wage) or an error in price (an opportunity); you rarely know which, and the answer decides whether it lasts.

                                          Why prices can be wrong: behaviour and limits to arbitrage

                                          Like you’re five

                                          Imagine you found a HK$100 note and then dropped a HK$100 note. Which feels worse? Almost everyone says dropping it. Losing hurts more than winning feels good. So people hold on to losers (“I will sell when it gets back to what I paid”) and rush to sell winners (“lock in the gain”), and they do it in crowds.

                                          The real idea

                                          Behavioural finance collects such systematic human biases: loss aversion (losses weigh about twice as much as equal gains, Kahneman and Tversky’s prospect theory), overconfidence, herding, anchoring, and the disposition effect (selling winners too early, holding losers too long). Move the sliders to shape the value function.

                                          Why don’t smart traders just remove the errors?

                                          Because arbitrage is limited. To bet against a mispricing you need capital, you pay borrowing and trading costs, you may be unable to short, and the error can grow before it shrinks, forcing you out with a loss. A saying often attributed to Keynes: “markets can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent”. The hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management (1998) is the classic case: its trades were right in the long run, but borrowed money (Lesson 12’s leverage) and a widening of the gaps forced it to unwind, and it had to be rescued by a consortium of banks. So mispricings can persist, but profiting from them needs patience, capital and survival.

                                          So where can an edge come from?

                                          The real idea

                                          Roughly five places, from the most to the least accessible:

                                          • Risk premia: being paid for bearing risk others avoid (market, value, momentum, carry, volatility). Not an “edge”, but a legitimate return.
                                          • Frictions and constraints: investors who have to trade (index funds rebalancing, forced sellers, mandate limits), creating predictable flows that you can supply liquidity to (Lesson 7).
                                          • Processing information better or faster: alternative data, text and satellite data, better models (the machine learning of Lesson 17), or microsecond speed (Lesson 7’s latency).
                                          • Behavioural errors: the persistent biases above, harvested systematically.
                                          • Structure and scale: small strategies too small for large funds to bother with; or a large one that others cannot copy.

                                          Individually each source is small. The fundamental law of active management (Grinold) says what matters is how many independent bets you make:

                                          IR ≈ TC × IC × √N

                                          The information ratio (IR) is the excess return per unit of risk, IC (information coefficient) is the correlation between your predictions and what happens (tiny, like 0.02 to 0.05), N the number of independent bets per year, and TC (transfer coefficient) the share of the signal that survives costs and constraints. A very small IC applied across thousands of independent bets can still give a good IR.

                                          Quant view

                                          The formula is the same 1/√N averaging from the first demo, now working for you. Two practical warnings. First, the bets must be independent: 500 stocks that all follow the market are not 500 bets, so N is an “effective” count (Lesson 9). Second, the law shows why quants focus on breadth, costs (which cut TC) and honest estimation of IC: a model whose backtested IC is 0.10 but whose true IC is 0.02 looks like a great strategy and loses money, which is the topic of the next two lessons.

                                          Quick check

                                          Must-know list

                                          • EMH: prices reflect available information. Weak (past prices), semi-strong (public information), strong (all). Efficient means unbiased, not always right.
                                          • The crowd’s average error shrinks like 1/√N only if errors are independent; herding breaks it.
                                          • Grossman–Stiglitz: perfect efficiency is impossible, since research must earn something. Markets are efficient enough.
                                          • Tests: autocorrelation with a ±1.96/√N band. Many tests means false alarms (multiple testing, p-hacking).
                                          • With N managers expect N/2Y with Y straight wins by luck. Watch for survivorship bias.
                                          • Anomalies (momentum, value, size, low volatility) decay after publication; too many factors, so demand a higher t-statistic.
                                          • Behavioural biases (loss aversion, overconfidence, herding) exist, but limits to arbitrage let mispricings persist and make exploiting them risky.
                                          • Fundamental law: IR ≈ TC × IC × √N. Small skill, many independent bets, low costs.

                                          Algorithmic trading and backtesting

                                          In one breath: an algorithmic strategy is a pipeline from data to signal to positions to orders, run by code under strict risk limits. You judge it by a backtest, a simulation on past data. A backtest is easy to build and very easy to fool: look-ahead, overfitting, ignored costs and luck can all make a worthless idea look brilliant. This lesson shows how a strategy works, how to measure it, and the traps that decide whether it survives contact with the real market.

                                          About 70 minutesNeeds: Lessons 7, 8, 10 and 15; basic programmingYou will learn: the strategy pipeline, a trend-following backtest, drawdowns, overfitting, Kelly sizing, transaction costs

                                            What an algorithmic strategy is

                                            Like you’re five

                                            An algorithmic trader writes down rules before the day starts, like a recipe: “if this happens, buy this much; if that happens, sell.” Then a computer follows the recipe without getting scared or greedy. The hard part is not following the recipe, it is finding a recipe that was good in the past for a real reason and will still be good tomorrow.

                                            The real idea

                                            Every systematic strategy, from a student’s script to a large fund, is the same five-stage pipeline:

                                            DataPrices, volumes, accounts, news, alternative data. Cleaned and stored point-in-time.
                                            SignalA forecast: a number per asset that predicts its return (the “alpha”).
                                            PortfolioTurn forecasts into target positions, sized by risk and costs (Lessons 9, 10).
                                            ExecutionBreak orders into pieces to trade cheaply (Lesson 7).
                                            Risk and monitoringLimits on size, loss and exposure. Can veto any stage, and shuts the system down when it misbehaves.

                                            Common families of signals: trend following or momentum (buy what has been rising), mean reversion (bet that stretched prices snap back), statistical arbitrage and pairs (trade the gap between related assets), carry (collect the yield or cost-of-carry difference, Lesson 12), market making (earn the spread, Lesson 7), and event-driven strategies around earnings or index changes. Execution algorithms such as TWAP and VWAP (spread an order over time, or in line with the day’s volume) do not make forecasts; they just lower the cost of trading.

                                            Your first backtest: trend following

                                            Like you’re five

                                            Look at a stock’s average price over the last 20 days and compare it with its average over the last 100 days. If the short average is above the long one, the price has recently been rising, so we buy and hold. If not, we sit in cash. That is it. The two averages are “moving averages”, and this “crossover” is the oldest trend-following rule there is.

                                            The real idea

                                            A backtest replays history: for each day, compute the signal using only data available up to that day, take the position, and record what the position earns the next day, minus costs. Two rules keep it honest:

                                            • No look-ahead: the signal on day t may only use data up to t, and the position it creates earns the return from t to t + 1.
                                            • Pay costs: every change of position pays a cost (spread, fees, slippage).

                                            The market below is simulated so that you control how trendy it is. With “trendiness” at 0 it is a pure random walk (Lesson 8): prices follow no pattern at all. Press “New market” several times and compare the strategy with simply holding.

                                            Trap

                                            A strategy that makes money in one backtest tells you almost nothing. On a pure random walk, an average-crossover rule still wins in about half the simulated markets, by luck. What matters is whether it beats holding consistently, across many markets, after costs, and for a reason you can explain (here: “trends persist”, which Lesson 15 told you is a weak, decaying effect).

                                            Scoring a strategy: return, risk and drawdown

                                            The real idea

                                            The standard scorecard (all from daily returns rt, annualised with 252 trading days):

                                            • Annual return (compound) and volatility = daily standard deviation × √252.
                                            • Sharpe ratio = mean excess return ÷ volatility, annualised (Lesson 10). Compare strategies per unit of risk.
                                            • Maximum drawdown: the largest fall from a previous peak to a later low, MDD = maxt (peakt − equityt)/peakt. It is what an investor actually lives through.
                                            • Turnover (how much you trade) and hit rate/profit factor, which show how a strategy earns its return.

                                            Drawdowns are bigger and longer than most beginners expect, even for a genuinely good strategy. Try it: generate histories with a given Sharpe ratio and look at how deep and how long the worst slump is.

                                            In the real world

                                            A strategy with a Sharpe ratio of 1 and 10% volatility will, in a typical 5-year stretch, still fall about 13% from a peak at some point (in one history out of ten, more than 20%), and can spend a year or more below its old high. Investors and risk managers cut strategies after such drawdowns, so a good strategy has to be small enough, or cheap enough, that you can survive its bad stretch. This is why drawdown limits are part of the pipeline.

                                            The big trap: overfitting

                                            Like you’re five

                                            A student gets the answers to last year’s exam and memorises them perfectly. On last year’s exam: 100%. On this year’s exam: very poor, because the memorised answers included luck and quirks that do not repeat. A trading rule tuned to fit past data is the same thing: it learned the quirks of one history, not a pattern that will last.

                                            The real idea

                                            Every strategy has dials (average lengths, thresholds, which stocks, which dates). If you try M settings and keep the best in-sample result, you select the one that was luckiest. Even if all settings are worthless, the best of M noisy Sharpe ratios grows with M: very roughly like √(2 ln M)/√T (an upper-end rule of thumb), where T is the years of data. The proof is out-of-sample: split the history into an in-sample part (where you search) and an out-of-sample part (untouched until you pick). The demo below searches crossover settings on a market that is, by construction, a pure random walk, so no setting has any real edge.

                                            Quant view

                                            Statistically, selecting the maximum of M estimates is a biased estimator: it overstates the truth, and the bias grows with M. The cure is to account for the search. Practical tools (more in Lesson 17): count every variant you try; use out-of-sample and walk-forward tests; prefer few parameters and a story; test across many assets and periods; apply a multiple-testing correction (such as the “deflated Sharpe ratio” of Bailey and López de Prado, which lowers a Sharpe ratio by the number of trials and the non-normality of returns). A good rule: the fewer the dials and the more the independent evidence, the more you can trust a backtest.

                                            A checklist of other backtest sins
                                            • Look-ahead bias: using information that was not yet known (for example, a company’s full-year accounts on 31 December, or the day’s closing price to decide a trade at the close).
                                            • Survivorship bias: testing only on stocks that exist today, ignoring the ones that went bankrupt or were delisted.
                                            • Ignoring costs and capacity: spread, fees, impact and borrow fees for shorting (next sections). A strategy trading small-caps may be impossible to run at size.
                                            • Unrealistic fills: assuming you always get the close price, or that you can short anything at any time.
                                            • Regime change: relationships break (interest-rate regimes, new regulation, other traders learning the same signal).

                                            How much to bet: position sizing

                                            Like you’re five

                                            You have a coin that lands heads 55% of the time, and you win what you bet when it does. If you bet everything each time, one tails ends the game. If you bet almost nothing, you never grow. There is a bet size in between that grows your money fastest in the long run, and betting more than that does worse, even though the coin is in your favour!

                                            The real idea

                                            The Kelly criterion (Kelly 1956, popularised by Ed Thorp) gives the fraction f of your wealth to bet that maximises the long-run growth rate g(f) = p ln(1 + bf) + (1 − p) ln(1 − f), for win probability p and a win that pays b times the stake:

                                            f* = p − 1 − pb

                                            The growth curve is lopsided: the loss of betting too much is steeper than the loss of betting too little. Betting twice Kelly gives zero growth, and more than that loses money despite the edge. And since the edge is never known exactly (Lesson 10: Sharpe estimates are noisy), practitioners bet a fraction, often half Kelly, which gives about three-quarters of the growth with far smaller swings. Drag the bet size.

                                            Quant view

                                            For continuous returns the same idea gives the leverage f* = (μ − r)/σ², the Sharpe ratio divided by the volatility. In practice funds use volatility targeting: size each position in proportion to 1/(forecast volatility), so each bet carries a similar amount of risk, then scale the whole book to a target volatility (say 10% a year). It is the portfolio idea of Lesson 9 turned into a rule: risk, not capital, is what you allocate.

                                            Costs: the silent strategy killer

                                            Like you’re five

                                            A shop earns HK$1 profit per item, but the delivery van charges HK$0.80 every trip. Sell a few items per trip and you make almost nothing. Trading is the same: every trade pays the spread, fees and the price you move by buying. A strategy that trades a lot needs a bigger edge per trade than one that trades rarely.

                                            The real idea

                                            One-way cost of a trade has two parts: half the bid-ask spread (Lesson 7), and the market impact of your own order pushing the price. A widely used rule of thumb, the square-root law, says the impact is about

                                            impact ≈ Y · σdaily · √(Q/V)

                                            where Q is your order, V the stock’s average daily volume and Y a constant of order 1. Doubling your size does not double the cost per share, it multiplies it by about 1.4, but the total cost grows faster than size. The annual drag is turnover × cost per trade. See how quickly a modest edge disappears.

                                            In the real world

                                            This is why the highest-turnover strategies (market making, high-frequency) invest in speed and low-cost access, and why a strategy that looks wonderful in a backtest without costs often has a negative live Sharpe ratio. For Hong Kong equities, the costs include stamp duty (charged on each side), exchange and clearing fees, brokerage and the spread, which are a heavier fixed burden for frequent traders. Short-selling adds borrowing fees, and is only allowed on designated securities. Always model the real fee schedule for the market you trade, and check the current rates.

                                            Quick check

                                            Must-know list

                                            • Pipeline: data → signal → portfolio → execution → risk. Families: trend, mean reversion, stat arb, carry, market making.
                                            • A backtest uses only information available at each date and pays costs. A single backtest proves little.
                                            • Scorecard: annual return, volatility, Sharpe, maximum drawdown, turnover. Drawdowns are deeper and longer than you expect.
                                            • Overfitting: the best of M tries is biased upward (≈ √(2 ln M)/√T in Sharpe). Check out-of-sample, count your trials, prefer few dials.
                                            • Other sins: look-ahead, survivorship bias, ignored costs and capacity, unrealistic fills, regime change.
                                            • Kelly: f* = p − (1 − p)/b. Over-betting hurts more than under-betting; use half Kelly and volatility targeting.
                                            • Costs = half-spread + impact (≈ Yσ√(Q/V)). Annual drag = turnover × cost per trade.

                                            Financial machine learning

                                            In one breath: machine learning in finance is not image recognition with price charts. The data is tiny compared with its noise, the world changes while you study it, labels overlap in time, and thousands of competitors are looking at the same numbers. The usual ML toolkit works only if you adapt it: heavy regularisation, careful features and labels, time-aware validation, and a deep suspicion of any result that looks too good.

                                              Why finance breaks the usual ML playbook

                                              Like you’re five

                                              Teaching a computer to recognise cats is easy: a cat looks like a cat today and tomorrow, there are millions of photos, and nobody is trying to hide cats from you. Predicting stocks is different. The “cat” changes shape every few months. You only have a few thousand days. The picture is mostly static (noise). And every other player is trying to spot the same cat first, and as soon as enough people do, it disappears.

                                              The real idea

                                              Four features set financial data apart from the data ML is usually built for:

                                              • Very low signal-to-noise ratio. Daily stock returns are almost entirely noise. A good model might explain well under 1% of the variance of next-day returns (R² of 0.1 to 0.5%). A tiny R² can still be a valuable edge (Lesson 15’s fundamental law) but it is hard to tell from nothing.
                                              • Little data. 20 years of daily data is only about 5,000 rows, and neighbouring days are correlated, so the effective sample is smaller. You cannot “just add more data”: history does not repeat to order.
                                              • Non-stationarity. The relationship between features and returns changes over time (regimes, other traders, regulation). Yesterday’s best model decays.
                                              • Competition (adversarial, self-undoing). Patterns that are easy to find get traded away (Lesson 15). Only the weak, costly or hidden ones remain.

                                              The first demo is the whole problem in miniature. Three features carry a real (tiny) signal; the other 57 are pure noise. Fit a regression with more and more features and compare the fit on the data it learned from (train) with the fit on fresh data (test). The R² numbers are in percent.

                                              Quant view

                                              In ordinary least squares with p useless features and N samples, the in-sample R² is inflated by about p/N, while the out-of-sample R² is reduced by about the same amount. When the true R² (the prize) is 1% and p/N is also about 1% (say 10 features on 1,000 days), the model is already giving it all back. This is the bias–variance trade-off with a very small signal: variance dominates, so heavy bias (strong regularisation, few features, simple models) wins. Try ridge regression: raising the penalty shrinks the noisy coefficients and rescues most of the test R².

                                              Features and labels done right

                                              Like you’re five

                                              A feature is a clue you could see before the race; the label is who won the race. If your clue was recorded after the race, even by accident, your “predictions” look fantastic and are worthless. In finance this is so easy to do by accident that it is the number one source of fake results.

                                              The real idea

                                              Features (the inputs X): trailing returns over several horizons (momentum), volatility, volume and liquidity measures, value and quality ratios from the accounts (Lesson 11, point-in-time!), cross-sectional ranks, market and sector context, and anything alternative (news sentiment, web traffic). Practical rules:

                                              • Use returns, not prices. Prices wander (non-stationary), returns are roughly stationary. Standardise features (z-scores), often cross-sectionally (rank the stocks against each other each day) to remove the market-wide level.
                                              • Lag everything. A feature stamped day t must use only information available when you can still trade on it. If you trade at the next open, that is the close of day t, not the close of day t + 1.
                                              • Fit scalers on training data only. Never standardise with the mean and standard deviation of the whole sample.

                                              Labels (the target y): the return over the next h days; the sign of it; the excess return over the market or sector; or a triple-barrier label (López de Prado): “did the price hit +2% first, −2% first, or neither within 10 days?” Predicting a relative or excess return is usually easier than predicting the raw return, since the market’s noise is removed.

r = prices.pct_change()
X = pd.DataFrame({'mom20': prices.pct_change(20),     # past 20-day return
                  'vol20': r.rolling(20).std(),        # past 20-day volatility
                  'rev5' : -prices.pct_change(5)})      # all known at the close of day t
y = prices.shift(-5) / prices - 1                    # return over the NEXT 5 days
data = pd.concat([X, y.rename('y')], axis=1).dropna()

                                              Leakage, and why ordinary cross-validation lies

                                              Like you’re five

                                              Suppose a student studies for a test by being shown a random half of last week’s weather reports, then is asked to guess the other half. Because Tuesday’s weather is almost the same as Monday’s, the student does very well just by looking at the neighbouring days. But that is not forecasting, it is copying from the next-door neighbour. Mixing days randomly between “training” and “testing” does exactly this to a financial model.

                                              The real idea

                                              Standard K-fold cross-validation assumes rows are independent. Financial rows are not: features change slowly (a 20-day average is nearly the same today and tomorrow) and labels overlap (the 10-day forward return from day t and from day t + 1 share 9 of their 10 days). Shuffle the rows, and almost every test row has a near-twin in the training set with a nearly identical label. A flexible model memorises this and reports skill that does not exist.

                                              The fixes: never shuffle time series; split in time order (train on the past, test on the future, a walk-forward test); and purge and embargo: remove training rows whose label window overlaps the test period, and a margin after it.                                               Trap

                                              Leakage rarely announces itself as an error. It shows up as a great result. A rule of thumb that has saved many quants: if the backtest looks too good (a Sharpe ratio above 2 or 3 for a daily-data model, hit rates over 55%), assume there is a bug until proven otherwise. Check the timestamps of every feature, the scaler, the label overlap and the data source before celebrating.

                                              A sound validation recipe
                                              1. Keep the last part of history as a final hold-out, untouched until one single test at the end.
                                              2. Inside the rest, use walk-forward or purged K-fold with an embargo to tune. (A variant, combinatorial purged CV, gives many paths for one model.)
                                              3. Choose hyper-parameters by nested validation, and count every model you try: the more you try, the larger the selection bias (Lesson 16).
                                              4. Evaluate with trading-relevant metrics, not just R²: information coefficient (the correlation of prediction and outcome, per period), the Sharpe ratio of the resulting portfolio after costs, turnover and drawdown.

                                              A changing world: non-stationarity

                                              Like you’re five

                                              You learn that “when the shop window shows a sale, customers come”. This was true last year. This year a new shop opened next door and customers do not care any more. If you keep trusting last year’s rule, you lose. If you only trust last week’s customers, a few random visitors fool you. How far back should you look? Too short is noisy, too long is out of date.

                                              The real idea

                                              In finance the best model parameters drift. The standard answer is re-estimation: refit on a rolling window (the last W days) or an expanding window, retraining regularly. The window length W is a bias–variance dial: short windows track change but give noisy estimates; long windows give stable estimates of an outdated truth. Here a hidden coefficient β slowly changes sign; a model fitted once is compared with one that is re-fitted on a rolling window.

                                              Which models, and what to expect

                                              The real idea
                                              • Regularised linear models (ridge, lasso, elastic net): the workhorse. Fast, stable, interpretable. Hard to beat when the signal is weak.
                                              • Gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost, LightGBM): the best generic choice for tabular features. They capture interactions and thresholds, but need shallow trees, small learning rates and early stopping.
                                              • Neural networks: useful with lots of data (order-book and tick data, text, many assets pooled together), tricky on daily data. Sequence models (LSTM, Transformers) on raw price histories have a poor record against simple baselines unless carefully constrained.
                                              • Ensembles and shrinkage: averaging many weak models reduces variance, the same 1/√N idea from Lesson 15. Pooling data across assets multiplies the sample size.
                                              • Reinforcement learning is attractive for execution and market making, where an environment can be simulated; for forecasting it mostly adds variance.

                                              Whatever the model, always compare it with a very simple benchmark (for example a single momentum or value signal). If a deep network cannot clearly beat a ridge regression out-of-sample, use the ridge regression.

                                              In the real world

                                              Published research on machine learning for stock returns (for example Gu, Kelly and Xiu, 2020, on US stocks) finds modest but real out-of-sample gains from non-linear models such as trees and neural networks over linear ones, with much of the predictive power coming from familiar signals: momentum, liquidity and volatility. Other studies (for example Avramov, Cheng and Metzker, 2022) find that the profits are concentrated in small, hard-to-trade stocks, which is just where costs bite hardest. The practical lesson is consistent: modest edges, a lot of care, and costs decide everything. Success usually comes from better data, cleaner labels and sounder validation rather than fancier models.

                                              Think about it

                                              Suppose your research process tries 500 feature and model combinations, and you pick the best one by its walk-forward result. Even if every combination is worthless, the best of 500 will look good (Lesson 16’s overfit demo). How would you protect yourself? Possible answers: a final hold-out used once, a deflated Sharpe ratio, requiring an economic reason for the feature before testing it, testing across many markets, and trading small first (paper trading) to confirm.

                                              Quick check

                                              Must-know list

                                              • Finance data: low signal-to-noise, little data, non-stationary, competitive. A daily-return R² of 0.1 to 0.5% can already be valuable.
                                              • Overfitting inflates in-sample R² by about p/N and deflates the out-of-sample R² by the same. Prefer few features, simple models, strong regularisation (ridge, lasso).
                                              • Features: returns not prices, standardised, lagged, point-in-time. Fit scalers on training data only.
                                              • Labels: forward return, excess return, sign, triple-barrier. Overlapping labels create serial correlation.
                                              • Never shuffle time series. Use walk-forward, purged K-fold with embargo, and a final untouched hold-out.
                                              • Too good to be true (Sharpe above 2 to 3 on daily data) means a bug until proven otherwise.
                                              • Non-stationarity: re-fit on a rolling window; the window is a bias–variance dial.
                                              • Models: regularised linear first, then boosted trees; deep nets need much more data. Always beat a simple benchmark, and judge by after-cost portfolio metrics.

                                                What you now know, in one picture

                                                The unifying idea

                                                All of finance is about cash flows over time. A price is today’s value of future cash flows, discounted for time (Lesson 3) and for risk (Lessons 8 to 10), and competition pushes prices toward that value (Lessons 7 and 15). Quantitative finance is the craft of estimating those cash flows, risks and mispricings honestly, with maths, code and data. Here is the course in ten principles:

                                                1. Money is a claim on real resources; banks create it by lending, and take on leverage and maturity risk (Lessons 1 and 2).
                                                2. A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. Value is the discounted sum of cash flows (3).
                                                3. Central banks and inflation drive interest rates, and rates drive every price (4, 5).
                                                4. Bonds and stocks are discounted expected cash flows. Company accounts are where those cash flows come from (5, 6, 11).
                                                5. Markets are matching engines. Spreads, liquidity and speed are real costs (7).
                                                6. Returns compound; risk is volatility and fat tails; diversification is the one free lunch (8, 9).
                                                7. Only risk you cannot diversify is paid (CAPM). Alpha is what remains after beta and factors (10).
                                                8. Derivatives are priced by replication and no-arbitrage, not by forecasts. Options are bets on volatility (12 to 14).
                                                9. Edges are rare, small and decaying. Luck looks like skill. Many independent bets, low costs (15).
                                                10. Backtests and ML models lie unless built with care: no look-ahead, honest validation, costs, regularisation, suspicion (16, 17).

                                                The project ladder

                                                Each rung proves a skill
                                                1. Look at real returns. Download Hang Seng Index and a few stocks. Reproduce Lesson 8: histograms against a normal curve, the fat tails, volatility clustering.
                                                2. Efficient frontier. Estimate means and covariances for 10 stocks, compute the frontier, then test it out of sample and see how estimation error hurts (Lesson 9).
                                                3. Factor strategy. Rank a universe by one signal (momentum or value), form long-short portfolios, and run an honest backtest with costs and walk-forward evaluation. Test against the standard factors (Lessons 10, 16).
                                                4. Options toolkit. Implement Black–Scholes, a binomial tree and a Monte Carlo pricer; compute Greeks and implied volatility; simulate a delta-hedged position (Lesson 14).
                                                5. Pairs or relative-value trade. Find related assets, test for cointegration, trade the spread, and measure how fast edges decay.
                                                6. ML cross-section. Predict next-month relative returns with ridge and boosted trees, using purged cross-validation, and judge by after-cost portfolio Sharpe and the deflated Sharpe ratio (Lesson 17).
                                                7. Paper trade. Run a simple strategy live on a paper-trading account for three months and compare with the backtest. The gap is your education.

                                                Habits of good quants

                                                What separates them
                                                • Count your trials. Keep a research log of every idea tried, not only the survivors. Selection bias is the quant’s original sin.
                                                • Start with the simplest model and make complexity earn its place out-of-sample.
                                                • Assume a bug first. A result that is too good is a bug until you have audited the timestamps, the labels and the costs.
                                                • Think in risk first, returns second: size positions to survive the bad stretch (Lesson 16).
                                                • Demand an economic reason for every signal. “It worked in the data” is not a reason.
                                                • Be humble about markets and curious about how they work. The people on the other side of your trade are often as clever as you.
